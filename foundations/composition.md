# Composition

## 1. What is it?

Composition is the art of arranging elements on a canvas so that the whole
communicates: deciding where the focal point is, how the eye travels, what leans on
what, and what stays empty. It operates at the scale of the entire artefact —
poster, screen, spread, slide — whereas the [elements](elements-of-design.md) are
its materials and the [principles](principles-of-design.md) its local relationships.
Rudolf Arnheim, whose *Art and Visual Perception* remains the foundational study,
described composition in terms of *perceptual forces*: every shape carries weight
and directionality, and a composition "works" when those forces settle into a
legible, purposeful equilibrium [canonical].

The topics this file owns are the compositional decisions themselves: focal points,
visual flow, symmetry versus asymmetry, dynamic versus static arrangement, layering,
cropping, negative space and visual tension. Structural mechanics — the column and
modular machinery that positions content — belong to [Grid
systems](../layout/grid-systems.md) and are linked there rather than repeated. The
distinction is practical: the grid answers "where do things sit?"; composition
answers "what should sit there, and what does the arrangement say?".

Composition is also the level at which designs acquire *character*. Two posters can
share identical typography, colour and grid and still feel entirely different — one
calm and official, one electric — purely because of compositional choices: where the
mass sits, how much is empty, whether the dominant axes are horizontal or diagonal.

## 2. Why does it matter?

Composition is the first thing perceived and the last thing consciously noticed.
Within the first seconds — before any text is read — a viewer has already located
the dominant mass, sensed the balance and registered the mood. NN/g's visual-design
research puts the evaluative test plainly: if users struggle to determine where to
look, the hierarchy is missing ("5 Principles of Visual Design in UX", 2020)
[accessed]. That "where to look" decision is compositional, and it gates everything
downstream: users who never find the focal point never read the message.

Composition matters for agents for a structural reason: it is the layer where fixed
assets (a logo, a photo, a headline) meet free decisions (placement, scale, space).
Extending a fixed event logo into a brand kit is mostly a compositional task — the
logo is given, and everything about *how it is presented* (clear space, dominance,
relationship to type, cropping) is composition. Good composition also scales down
gracefully: a poster's hierarchy survives at thumbnail size, which is exactly how
most people first encounter event graphics in feeds and listing grids.

Finally, composition is where design departs from decoration in a verifiable way.
Focal points can be tested (what was seen first?), flow can be traced (what did the
eye do next?), tension can be diagnosed (what pulls against what?). This makes
composition one of the few "artistic" layers that an agent can evaluate objectively,
using the tools in [Design critique](design-critique.md).

## 3. Core principles

### 3.1 Focal points

A focal point is the element designed to be seen first — the composition's entry
point. It is created by weight: the combination of scale, value contrast, isolation,
colour intensity and, sometimes, direction from neighbouring elements pointing at
it. Arnheim's analysis of visual weight shows why position matters as much as size:
a mark near the *centre* of the canvas carries more weight than the same mark near
the edge, and the centre is the strongest position in the perceptual field (*The
Power of the Center*) [canonical]. NN/g's UX framing agrees on the diagnostic: one
element should clearly win the "where do I look" race [accessed].

Most compositions need exactly one primary focal point; secondary focal points are
acceptable only when a clear rank exists. The failure modes are symmetrical: *no
focal point* (evenly weighted mosaics that read as wallpaper) and *competing focal
points* (two elements of similar weight fighting for first look — the eye shuttles
and the message halves). A robust test: show the design for five seconds, remove it,
ask what was seen first. If the answer is not the intended element, the composition
has a focal problem before it has any other problem.

### 3.2 Visual flow

Visual flow is the designed path the eye takes after the entry point: entry →
primary message → supporting content → action or sign-off. Flow is built from four
instruments: *directional* cues (diagonals, curves, arrows, gazes and pointed
shapes), *rhythmic* cues (repeated units at regular intervals that march the eye
along), *value* gradients (light-to-dark progressions lead in reading direction),
and *spatial* cues (alignment lines that carry the eye from block to block). Reading
culture sets the baseline: left-to-right scripts bias flow toward the top-left
entry; right-to-left scripts mirror it.

Flow is evaluated by tracing, not by taste: squint until only the masses remain,
then follow them in order; or use the five-second test with a follow-up question
("what did you see *second*?"). A composition with hierarchy but no flow behaves
like a one-line message — strong at entry, silent after. A composition with
*contradictory* flow (several diagonals aiming at different targets) makes the eye
orbit and read nothing in order. The scanning research behind these habits — how
users actually move through text-heavy layouts — is treated in [Gestalt and
perception](gestalt-and-perception.md); the compositional task here is to *direct*
that scanning rather than leave it to default.

### 3.3 Symmetry and asymmetry

Symmetry mirrors content around an axis; asymmetry balances unequal weights at
unequal distances. They are not a good/bad pair but a signalling pair. Symmetry
reads as formal, calm, official and monumental — appropriate for certificates,
religious and civic buildings' graphics, luxury packaging and anything that must
feel *settled*. Asymmetry reads as modern, energetic and deliberate — appropriate
for most contemporary brand and editorial work. Radial symmetry (content arranged
around a centre) is a third option, useful for seals, badges and celebratory marks.

The professional subtlety is that *asymmetry still requires balance*: asymmetry is
not imbalance. The small dark counterweight in the corner opposite the huge headline
is what makes the page feel resolved rather than lopsided (Arnheim's
perceptual-balance analysis) [canonical]; NN/g's balance guidance likewise describes
distributing visual signal evenly "not necessarily symmetrically" [accessed]. The
failure mode to avoid is *near-symmetry* — layouts that almost mirror but drift,
which reads as error rather than either calm or energy.

### 3.4 Dynamic composition

Dynamic composition implies motion, instability and time. Its instruments are
diagonal dominance, asymmetric balance, scale contrast (one very large element
against small ones), cropped or bleeding elements (part of the subject outside the
frame implies a larger world in motion), and directional whitespace. Dynamic
compositions suit messages about action, change, urgency and youth: event posters,
sports branding, campaign pages, bold hero sections.

Dynamics cost control. Diagonals and bleeds reduce the resting stability that dense
informational content needs, so dynamic compositions must work harder on hierarchy
to stay legible. The classic misuse is dynamics as noise: several diagonals, several
bleeds and competing scale jumps, none of which organise anything. Dynamic does not
mean chaotic — the strongest dynamic posters are rigorously constructed, with every
diagonal serving the path from focal point to call to action.

### 3.5 Static composition

Static composition implies stability, order and permanence. Its instruments are
horizontal and vertical dominance, symmetrical or gently asymmetric balance,
generous and even spacing, and contained (non-bleeding) elements. It suits messages
about trust, precision, heritage and ceremony: annual reports, legal and policy
documents, institutional home pages, certificates.

Statics have their own failure mode: dullness. A static composition that merely
avoids dynamics becomes dead rather than calm; the craft lies in *restraint plus one
measured activation* — a single warm accent, one small rotation, one oversized
numeral — that keeps the composition alive without disturbing its settlement.
Choosing static versus dynamic is a communication decision, not a taste one; the
decision framework in section 6 turns it into a checklist question.

### 3.6 Layering

Layering organises content into planes of depth — foreground, midground, background
— using overlap, occlusion, scale and focus. In print, layering appears as collage,
image-over-type, and die-cut reveals. On screen, layering is literal: elevation
systems, overlays, modals and parallax are figure–ground organisation applied
dynamically. Perceptually, layering works through *occlusion* — an object partially
covering another is read as nearer — which makes overlap the strongest depth cue
available (the figure–ground mechanism is described in [Gestalt and
perception](gestalt-and-perception.md)).

Layering serves two functions: depth (realism, focus) and *ordering of attention*
(what is on top is read first). Its misuse is depth anarchy: many semi-transparent
layers with ambiguous occlusion, where the viewer cannot resolve what is in front;
and decorative layering that buries content — text over busy imagery without a
scrim, or a background element that repeatedly edges into the foreground's weight.
The rule is one clear winner per region: every area of the canvas should resolve
instantly to "this plane is in front".

### 3.7 Cropping

Cropping is the decision of what to include, exclude and cut at the frame edge.
Three effects matter. *Simplification*: cropping removes detail to concentrate
meaning — a tight crop of hands at work says more than the full room. *Implication*:
bleeding an element off the canvas implies continuation beyond the frame, adding
scale and energy. *Completion*: viewers finish partially shown shapes (closure), so
a three-quarter face can read as more engaging than a full one — but only up to a
limit: crops that sever a face at the chin, a logo at a critical contour or a
product at its identifying feature prevent completion and read as accidents
(closure's sufficiency condition is covered in [Gestalt and
perception](gestalt-and-perception.md)).

Professional cropping conventions worth respecting: never crop faces at joints or
mid-features; protect identifying marks; keep text out of the crop edge; and respect
print bleed and safe areas so the intended crop, not the printer's tolerance,
decides what is cut. Cropping also interacts with brand work — a logo supplied with
clear-space rules must never be cropped at all, making cropping decisions part of
brand-kit governance.

### 3.8 Negative space

In composition, negative space is the designed emptiness that shapes the whole: the
margin that frames the focal point, the channel that separates the columns, the
clearing that lets a logo breathe. Betty Edwards' drawing pedagogy famously trains
students to draw the *spaces between* objects precisely because attending to
negative space reveals form more reliably than attending to objects (*Drawing on the
Right Side of the Brain*) [canonical]. The compositional insight is the same: what
surrounds a subject determines how it reads.

Negative space does four compositional jobs: it *isolates* (creating emphasis and
dominance), *connects* (an implied arrow of emptiness between two blocks reads as
movement), *frames* (a margin is the cheapest way to make content feel intentional),
and *paces* (emptiness is the beat between content masses). It is also a brand
deliverable: clear-space specifications around marks are negative space turned into
a rule. The two failure modes are crowding (no shape to the space — everything feels
jammed) and *trapped space* (awkward enclosed gaps between elements that read as
holes), usually the result of element-by-element placement rather than whole-canvas
planning.

### 3.9 Visual tension

Visual tension is the felt push-and-pull between compositional forces: a shape
leaning toward the edge, two masses almost touching, a diagonal resisting a
horizontal. Tension is energy — it keeps a composition alive, directs attention and
creates the "almost" moments that eyes love to resolve. Arnheim treats these forces
as the substance of composition: perceived directionality and weight *are* the
tension field, and skilled designers compose with them the way composers compose
with harmonic tension [canonical].

Tension needs management. *Productive* tension is resolved by the composition itself
— the eye travels the path and settles at the intended resting point (usually the
focal point). *Unproductive* tension is never resolved: an element uncomfortably
close to the canvas edge, a shape whose alignment nearly matches another, a margin
that is almost but not quite even — the viewer feels unease and reads error. The
diagnostic question is whether each felt tension leads somewhere (to the focal
point, to resolution) or merely exists. Deliberate tension plus one resolution reads
as mastery; accidental tension with no resolution reads as incompetence.

## 4. How it works

Composition works because perception turns positions into forces. Four weight
factors dominate. *Position*: centre is strongest, top heavier than bottom, and in
left-to-right cultures the left tends to be "entered" first. *Scale*: bigger is
heavier. *Contrast*: higher value/colour contrast is heavier. *Density*: textured,
detailed areas outweigh empty ones. Every placement decision re-weights the whole
canvas — moving one element changes the balance of all others, which is why
composition cannot be patched locally.

Around these forces, perception builds structure. The eye seeks an entry (the
strongest weight), then follows the paths the arrangement affords (alignment lines,
diagonals, gradients), then rests where forces resolve. Arnheim additionally
describes a *structural net* — the compositional skeleton of a canvas: its centre,
diagonals, axes and quadrants — whose strong positions attract weight; designers
place dominant content on or near these lines, or deliberately off them to create
tension [canonical]. Rule-of-thirds placement is a simplified professional
convention derived from this logic — a useful starting point, not a law, and worth
breaking when centrality serves the message.

The consequence for practice: compose in *masses* before composing in details.
Squint-reduced sketches (or reduced thumbnails) show only the weights and paths — if
the masses already tell the story, the details will survive; if they do not, no
detail work will save the design.

## 5. Practical application

A repeatable composition workflow for posters, hero pages and document covers:

1. **Define the message hierarchy in one sentence.** "This poster's job is: viewer sees event name → date → where to act." The sentence defines the focal point and the flow targets before any placement begins.
2. **Choose the character: static or dynamic.** Match it to the communication goal (framework below), and pick the dominant axes accordingly (horizontal/vertical versus diagonal).
3. **Block the masses.** Place the primary mass, then balance it with secondary masses and negative space. Work with grey blocks at thumbnail size; verify balance with the squint test.
4. **Set the entry and the path.** Make the focal point win the five-second test; then check what is seen second and third — adjust gradients, alignments and diagonals until the traced path matches the sentence from step 1.
5. **Decide the frame.** What bleeds, what is contained, what is cropped. Protect faces, marks and text; use bleed deliberately for implication, not habitually.
6. **Tune tension.** Remove unresolved tensions (near-alignments, edge-crowding); keep one or two deliberate ones that steer the eye. Verify every tension resolves at the focal point or along the path.
7. **Stress-test reductions.** Thumbnail, greyscale, squint. Composition that survives reduction survives the feed, the corridor and the cheap print run.

For interface work, the same workflow compresses: the viewport is the canvas, the
hero or primary action is the focal mass, and scroll supplies the flow path;
layering (elevation, overlays) follows the figure–ground rule of one clear winner
per region.

## 6. Decision framework

| Decision | Choose... | When the message is... | Evidence/rationale |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Focal strategy | Single dominant | One primary action or message | NN/g: users must know where to look; one element should win the glance [accessed] |
| | Mosaic (no single focus) | Gallery walls, equal-status item sets | Legitimate when items are genuinely equal rank |
| Balance | Symmetrical | Formal, ceremonial, stable, official | Symmetry signals order and settlement [canonical] |
| | Asymmetrical | Modern, energetic, confident | Requires deliberate counterweights; NN/g balance types [accessed] |
| Character | Static | Trust, precision, heritage, dense info | Horizontal/vertical dominance, contained elements |
| | Dynamic | Action, urgency, youth, campaigns | Diagonals, bleeds, scale contrast [canonical] |
| Crop behaviour | Contained | Documentation, controls, logos | Identity marks must never be cropped |
| | Bleeding | Energy, scale, implication of a larger world | Closure completes the cut form when sufficient cues remain |
| Space budget | Generous | Premium, calm, low-density messages | Emptiness signals confidence; enables isolation |
| | Economical | Dense reference content | Grouping systems must compensate for density |
| Tension | One deliberate conflict | Posters, heroes, campaign art | Tension resolves at the focal point |
| | Minimal | Forms, documents, dashboards | Unresolved tension reads as error in task contexts |

When two goals conflict (a playful event brand on a formal invitation), resolve by
the artefact's primary job: what must the viewer *do* — and let composition serve
that job, with the losing character expressed in smaller details.

## 7. Examples

**Poster — fixed logo as focal partner.** The event logo (given asset) is placed at
a strong structural position — upper-left entry for LTR audiences — with mandated
clear space turning negative space into brand governance. The composition is
dynamic: one diagonal image band cuts from logo to date block, bleeding off the
right edge to imply continuation. Balance is asymmetrical: the heavy lower-right
mass of the image is counterweighted by the small, dark, isolated ticket URL at
upper-left's opposite. Tension: the band's diagonal is the one deliberate conflict;
everything else is aligned. Reduction test: at thumbnail size the masses still read
— logo clearing, diagonal, dark counterweight.

**Dashboard hero — static restraint.** The primary metric is the focal point:
largest scale, strongest value contrast, isolated by space at the structural
centre-left. Flow descends in reading order to the supporting chart, then to the
action row. No diagonals, no bleeds; tension limited to one accent colour. The
composition deliberately borrows static character because the user's task is
precision, not excitement.

**Document cover — symmetrical formality.** Title set centrally on the vertical
axis, generous margins all round, contained imagery, no cropping of any mark. The
only activation is a single rule line in the brand accent. This is the static
character at its most ceremonial — appropriate for annual reports and certificates,
where symmetry *is* the message.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Two focal points.** Equal-weight hero elements competing for the first glance; the eye shuts down and reads neither.
- **Asymmetry without balance.** Confusing lopsidedness with dynamism; the counterweight is missing, so the composition reads as accidental.
- **Diagonal inflation.** Many diagonals pointing at different targets; flow shatters and the eye orbits.
- **Decorative bleed.** Cropping everything off-edge "for energy" until nothing is contained and the frame loses meaning.
- **Cruel crops.** Faces at joints, logos at contours, products at identifying features — closure prevented, readability damaged.
- **Trapped space.** Awkward enclosed gaps that read as holes; usually a symptom of element-by-element placement.
- **Unresolved tension.** Near-alignments, edge-crowding and almost-even margins that feel like errors because they never resolve.
- **Ambiguous layering.** Semi-transparent planes that never declare which is in front; figure–ground stays unresolved.
- **Detail-first composing.** Styling elements before masses are placed; the polish then protects a broken structure.
- **Copy-composition mismatch.** A dynamic arrangement for a trust message, or a static grid for a campaign — character contradicting content.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- State the focal point in one sentence; confirm a five-second viewer would name it first.
- Trace the eye path from entry to goal; does it match the intended message order? Any contradictory directional cues?
- Squint: do the masses balance around the intended axis? Is the dominant axis (horizontal/vertical/diagonal) consistent with the message?
- Is the character choice (static/dynamic, symmetric/asymmetric) a deliberate communication decision, documented in the brief?
- Does every bleed and crop protect faces, marks, text and identifying features? Do cropped forms still complete (closure)?
- Is negative space shaped — does every gap separate, frame, connect or pace something? Any trapped holes?
- List the felt tensions; does each resolve at the focal point or along the path? Remove any that do not.
- Are layers decisive — does every region resolve instantly to a clear front plane?
- Does the composition survive thumbnail, greyscale and squint reductions?
- Is near-symmetry absent — either fully mirrored or clearly asymmetric?
