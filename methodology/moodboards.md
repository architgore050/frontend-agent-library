# Moodboards

## 1. What is it?

A moodboard is a curated collection of visual references — images, colour swatches, type specimens, layout fragments, textures — assembled not to be copied but to make a *visual direction* concrete and discussable before any real design work begins. A good moodboard answers a question: "if we go in this direction, what will our design look and feel like?" A bad one answers a different question: "here is a pile of images I like."

Moodboards are the central instrument of the divergent Develop phase (see [Design thinking](design-thinking.md)). They sit between discovery and production: discovery (see [Research and discovery](research-and-discovery.md)) produces implications and constraints; the moodboard turns candidate answers to those implications into visual form; production then executes the chosen direction under rules extracted from it.

## 2. Why does it matter?

Language is a poor medium for visual direction. "Warm, crafted, slightly retro but modern" means something different to every person who reads it, and stakeholders only discover their disagreement after the design is finished — the most expensive possible moment. A moodboard moves that discovery to the cheapest moment: it makes each direction concrete, so stakeholders react to *something they can see* while the cost of changing course is still near zero.

Moodboards also protect against two process failures. First, premature convergence: building boards for three distinct directions forces the exploration of alternatives before one wins (see [Ideation](ideation.md)). Second, untraceable style drift: because the board's attributes are extracted into explicit rules, every later decision can be checked against the agreed direction instead of the designer's mood.

## 3. Core principles

- **References are data, not destinations.** Each reference is interrogated for *why* it works — which attributes produce its effect — never treated as a design to replicate.
- **Attributes over artefacts.** The unit of meaning on a moodboard is the attribute (a type treatment, a palette relationship, a density level), not the artefact (a poster you found). Artefacts illustrate attributes.
- **Distinct directions, equal effort.** Explore genuinely different directions at comparable fidelity, so stakeholders choose between concepts rather than between levels of polish.
- **Harmony with the fixed constraints.** Every direction must be built to honour the fixed elements (a locked logo, mandated colours, brand attributes); the direction varies the *treatment*, not the constraints.
- **Annotation is the board.** A board without captions is a collage. Each reference needs one line saying what attribute it demonstrates. The captions are what make the board reviewable and the rules extractable.
- **Provenance and rights.** Record where each reference came from, and treat all of it as analysis material only — reference images are not assets you ship (see [Image rights](../imagery/image-rights.md)).

## 4. How it works

### 4.1 How to gather references

1. **Start from the brief, not from aesthetics.** Convert the discovery implications into search axes: audience descriptors, brand personality words (see [Brand personality](../branding/brand-personality.md)), sector, medium, and the fixed logo's own attributes. Generate concrete search terms per axis ("music festival poster, flat colour, bold condensed type").
2. **Source deliberately.** Use design galleries, the sector's real output (competitor artefacts for analysis), photography libraries, and your own generated candidates. Keep a provenance note (source URL or filename) with every image; you will need it for rights review and for explaining your reasoning.
3. **Collect wide, then prune.** Gather two to three times what you need, then cut anything that does not clearly demonstrate an attribute you want. If you cannot say what attribute an image is on the board for, it does not go on.
4. **Mind the licensing boundary.** Found images may be viewed and analysed; they may not be shipped. Generated reference images have their own rights questions (see [AI image generation](../imagery/ai-image-generation.md) and [AI-assisted design](ai-assisted-design.md)).
5. **Include the negatives.** Sometimes the most useful reference is one that shows what to avoid ("this is what over-decorating looks like for this audience"). Mark it explicitly as an anti-reference.

### 4.2 How many directions to explore

The working convention in this library — and a common studio practice — is **three distinct directions per moodboard round**, and the number matters more than it looks:

- **One** direction is not exploration; it is the first idea seeking approval, and stakeholders who dislike it must restart the conversation from nothing.
- **Two** creates a binary choice, which pushes discussion toward "A or B?" and usually rewards the slightly-more-polished option rather than the better concept.
- **Three** creates genuine comparison: it reveals what the *common core* is (the attributes all directions share, which are probably the real requirements) and what is genuinely optional (the differences). Three also lets you role a portfolio of risk: typically one **safe/refined** direction (closest to sector convention), one **expressive/bold** direction (pushes personality), and one **unconventional** direction (challenges a convention on purpose, so its cost can be discussed). That spread is a convention, not a law — label it as such and adapt to context.
- **Four or more** dilutes attention; stakeholders compare images instead of concepts, and feedback quality drops. If you truly cannot decide between four, you have not defined the attributes sharply enough.

Each direction needs comparable craft: same number of references, same annotation depth, applied to the same sample artefact. Uneven effort is a hidden vote.

### 4.3 How to identify common visual characteristics

Once references are assembled, extract attributes systematically across fixed axes. For each axis, describe what the references actually do, then what the direction should do.

| Attribute axis | What to look at | Questions to answer per direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type treatment | Classification (serif/sans/slab/display), weight range, case, tracking, scale contrast between headings and body | Does type lead the design or support it? How loud is the loudest type? What spacing rhythm? |
| Palette | Hue families, saturation, value range, proportion (dominant/secondary/accent), contrast level | Which relationships recur? Is contrast high or restrained? Does any colour carry symbolic load (see [Colour psychology](../colour/colour-psychology.md))? |
| Density and space | Amount of content per area, margin generosity, whitespace as an active element | Is the feel dense/compressed or airy? Where does breathing room concentrate? |
| Shape language | Corners, line weight, geometric vs organic curves, edge treatment, recurring motifs | Sharp or soft? Does the language echo the fixed logo's shapes? |
| Imagery treatment | Photo vs illustration vs abstract, cropping, duotone/flat treatment, grain and texture | Realistic or stylised? Warm or cool processing? Consistent frame or full-bleed? |
| Composition logic | Alignment, symmetry/asymmetry, grid feel, hierarchy device (size, weight, position, colour) | Orderly or dynamic? What carries the eye first (see [Composition](../foundations/composition.md))? |

Two extraction passes make this rigorous. **Within one direction:** what do its references share? The overlap is the direction's signature — write it as 5–8 attribute statements. **Across directions:** what do all boards share? That overlap is the *requirement set* — attributes every candidate must keep because discovery showed they are non-negotiable (usually harmony with the fixed logo and honesty to the brand's personality).

### 4.4 How to avoid copying reference designs

Copying is the default failure mode of moodboards. The defence is the **abstraction step**: never carry an artefact forward; carry only the attribute, converted into a rule.

The chain is: **reference → attribute → rule → application.**

1. **Reference:** "I like this festival poster" — a specific artefact by someone else.
2. **Attribute:** "its energy comes from oversized condensed type cropped at the edges and a two-colour palette" — a property, not a thing.
3. **Rule:** "headlines use a condensed sans at 2.5–3× the next largest text, set in two colours from the kit palette, and may be cropped at the container edge" — an original, checkable rule.
4. **Application:** the rule applied to *our* content, *our* typeface, *our* palette produces something no reference contains.

Guardrails while abstracting:

- Extract at least two independent attributes from any reference you lean on; a design that takes only one attribute from one source is usually still recognisably that source.
- Combine attributes across *unrelated* references — the recombination is where originality lives.
- Change the category of every borrowed device: if the reference crops type, decide what *you* crop or scale; if it uses duotone photography, decide whether duotone serves your content at all.
- Run the "swap test": mentally substitute your fixed logo and palette into the reference's layout. If the result still looks like the reference, you extracted decoration, not attributes.
- Record the chain in the decision log for any rule that came from an obvious reference — it proves originality and documents reasoning (see [Brand guidelines](../branding/brand-guidelines.md) on decision documentation).

### 4.5 How to translate a moodboard into actual design rules

A moodboard becomes useful when its attribute statements are converted into **rules precise enough to build from and evaluate against**. Rules have a home in the wider system (see [Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md) for the token layer and [Visual identity](../branding/visual-identity.md) for the identity layer); at moodboard level they are directional but must already be checkable.

Rule format — each rule names the attribute, the decision, and a value or test:

```text
Attribute: type leads the design.
Rule:     Primary headings: weight 700, 48–64px on web (3.5–4× body);
          sentence case; tracking normal. Body: weight 400, 16–18px, 1.6 line-height.
Check:    No heading below 3× body scale; no third weight family in the kit.

Attribute: restrained two-colour palette with one warm accent.
Rule:     Surfaces: off-white #FAF7F2 and ink #1E2733. Accent: amber #E8A13A,
          max ~10% of any composition, never below WCAG AA contrast for text
          (verify each pairing — see Accessible colour).
Check:    Count accent area per artefact; run contrast maths on every text pair.
```

Rules split into **hard rules** (contrast thresholds, fixed logo clear space, mandated fonts — checkable by machine) and **soft rules** (density feel, imagery warmth — checkable by the attribute questions in §4.3 during critique). Every rule should say how it will be checked; a rule without a check is a preference.

### 4.6 How to present alternatives

- **Same content everywhere.** Apply each direction to the identical sample artefact (a badge, a hero section, a poster mock) so the comparison isolates the direction, not the content.
- **Equal fidelity.** Polish all directions equally. Unpolished directions get punished for the wrong reason.
- **Side-by-side, neutral framing.** Present all directions together, with a one-line attribute summary per direction ("Direction B: condensed type leads, high contrast, flat imagery"). Never reveal your favourite first or frame any option as "the safe one" out loud — that is steering, and it corrupts the feedback you need.
- **Show the common core explicitly.** State what every direction shares (the requirement set) before showing what differs. This lets stakeholders react to the real decision.
- **State the trade-off of each direction.** "Direction C breaks the sector's dark-theme convention: more distinctive, higher comprehension risk." Stakeholders can only choose well if they know what each choice costs.

### 4.7 How to obtain meaningful stakeholder feedback

The rule: **ask about attributes, never about taste.** "Which do you like?" invites opinion about moodboards as objects, rewards surface appeal, and produces feedback you cannot act on. Attribute questions produce statements you can turn into rules.

**Stakeholder feedback question set** (use per direction, then across directions):

1. **Personality check:** "If this event/brand were a person, how would this direction change how you describe them?" (tests whether the direction transmits the intended [brand personality](../branding/brand-personality.md))
2. **Attribute ranking:** "Rank these words for how well this direction expresses them: modern / traditional, playful / serious, energetic / calm, exclusive / welcoming." (comparable across directions)
3. **Fit check:** "Does anything here feel wrong for our audience? Which reference comes closest to feeling wrong, and why?"
4. **Constraint check:** "Look at the fixed logo on each board. Which direction makes it feel most at home? Does any direction fight it?"
5. **Application check:** "Imagine this on a badge, on the website header, on a banner. Which direction would survive all three best?"
6. **Difference probe:** "Direction A and B share most attributes — what is the one thing you would keep from each?" (extracts the synthesis you may have missed)
7. **Anti-reference check:** "Is anything here too close to another brand you know? Which?" (catches copying that internal review missed)
8. **Commitment probe:** "If we built everything from direction X, what would you expect to see change in six months?" (surfaces durability concerns)

Feedback handling: record all answers against attribute axes, mark each comment as *objective defect*, *direction conflict* (contradicts the requirement set), or *preference*, and resolve accordingly — defects get fixed, conflicts get discussed against objectives, preferences get weighed but do not win by volume.

## 5. Practical application

### 5.1 Worked example — moodboard to rules (event logo extension)

Scenario: a fixed event logo (geometric sans wordmark, deep navy #1B2A4A, amber accent #E8B23A — illustrative values sampled from the supplied file) must extend into a kit for a two-day design conference. Discovery implications: mobile-first audience, price transparency, differentiation against the sector's dark-theme convention.

Three directions, each applied to the same badge mock-up:

- **Direction A — Editorial clarity (safe/refined).** Light off-white surfaces, navy text, amber accents ≤10%; generous margins; photography in navy duotone. Attributes: calm, information-first, type supports.
- **Direction B — Type-led energy (expressive).** Condensed headings at 3–4× body scale, cropped headline composition, flat two-colour illustration, higher saturation. Attributes: loud, contemporary, type leads.
- **Direction C — Tactile print heritage (unconventional).** Warm paper tones, slab-serif accents, visible grid, textured photography; deliberately echoes print-conference heritage against the digital convention. Attributes: crafted, warm, denser.

Cross-direction common core (the requirement set): navy must stay dominant for logo harmony; amber works only as accent with verified contrast; geometry from the logo echoes in the shape language; mobile-first density. Directions differ on type voice, imagery treatment, and warmth.

The chosen direction (B, on grounds of audience energy and differentiation) extracts to rules:

| Attribute | Rule | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type leads | Headings: condensed sans, 700, 3–4× body scale; one accent weight (600) for subheads; no additional families | Scale ratio check per artefact |
| Two-colour restraint | Surfaces off-white/navy; amber ≤10% area; amber never used for body text | Area + contrast check |
| Flat illustration | Illustration over photography for mood imagery; photography (real people/venue) allowed, navy duotone treatment | Asset review at [Image selection](../imagery/image-selection.md) |
| Logo echo | Corner radius and stroke weights match the logo's geometry (sampled from file) | Visual comparison at critique |
| Mobile-first density | Compose at 375px first; desktop extends the same grid | Responsive check (see [Responsive layouts](../layout/responsive-layouts.md)) |

Each rule then feeds the kit build (tokens in [Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md), documentation in [Brand guidelines](../branding/brand-guidelines.md)) and is enforced during [Design critique](design-critique.md).

## 6. Decision framework

| Situation | Recommended action |
| --- | --- |
| New brand, no fixed assets | Three full directions from personality attributes; the logo itself is a direction output, not a constraint |
| Fixed logo, extend to kit (this library's reference case) | Extract the logo's attributes first (see [Fixed logo analysis](../branding/fixed-logo-analysis.md)); directions vary treatment while sharing the harmony core |
| Client says "just show us something" | Show one direction only if it is explicitly a probe, not a decision point — state that alternatives exist |
| Stakeholders deadlocked between two directions | Re-run attribute ranking questions (§4.7) against objectives; do not average the designs |
| Direction must survive many media | Add the application check (§4.7 Q5) and test on the hardest medium first |
| Time is minimal | Compress to: attribute axes → three small boards → ranking questions; never skip the abstraction step |

## 7. Examples

- The §5.1 worked example shows the full chain for the event-kit scenario: audit → three directions → common core → rules → checks.
- **Anti-example (copying):** a board built around one admired poster, whose "direction" is that poster with different words. The swap test fails: insert the real logo and it still looks like the poster. Corrected by extracting *two* attributes (cropped condensed type, two-colour restraint) and recombining with attributes from an unrelated editorial reference.
- **Anti-example (taste voting):** a stakeholder picks the direction whose sample photo is prettiest. Corrected by re-anchoring on attribute-ranking answers, which revealed the prettiest board transmitted the wrong personality (exclusive, where the audience needed welcoming).

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Collage without captions.** Boards of unexplained images cannot be reviewed or converted into rules.
- **One direction dressed as three.** Three boards that share every attribute are one direction; make the differences structural (type voice, density, imagery), not cosmetic.
- **Steering the vote.** "I'd probably go with B" before feedback is collected biases everything after it. Present neutrally, then give your recommendation with reasons after.
- **Unequal effort across directions.** The polished board wins for the wrong reason; budget effort per direction and stick to it.
- **Extracting decoration instead of attributes.** Taking the reference's textures but missing the *why* (contrast structure, hierarchy device). Run the attribute axes on every strong reference.
- **Shipping reference imagery.** Found images are analysis material; rights questions live elsewhere (see [Image rights](../imagery/image-rights.md)).
- **Ignoring the fixed constraints.** A direction that fights the locked logo is not an option, however attractive; surface the conflict instead of hiding it.
- **Unverified colour claims.** Hex values on boards must be sampled or verified, and contrast claims must be computed (see [AI-assisted design](ai-assisted-design.md) on hallucinated specs).

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- [ ] Every reference has a one-line annotation naming the attribute it demonstrates, plus provenance.
- [ ] Exactly three directions (or a stated, justified alternative count), structurally distinct, equal effort.
- [ ] The cross-direction common core is written down as a requirement set.
- [ ] Each direction declares harmony with fixed constraints (logo sampled values, mandated colours).
- [ ] The abstraction chain (reference → attribute → rule → application) is documented for any rule derived from a strong reference.
- [ ] Rules are checkable: hard rules have measurable values, soft rules have attribute questions.
- [ ] All directions were presented side-by-side on the same sample artefact, with trade-offs stated.
- [ ] Feedback was collected with the attribute question set; each comment classified as defect / conflict / preference.
- [ ] No shipped asset originates from a reference image.
