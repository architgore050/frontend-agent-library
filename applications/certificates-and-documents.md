# Certificates and formal documents

## 1. What is it?

This page covers formal print documents: certificates (awards, participation, completion), letterheads, memos and official correspondence — single or multi-page artifacts whose visual job is to **confer authority and record facts**. Where a poster earns attention and a slide supports speech, a formal document earns *trust*: the design must signal legitimacy (institution, credential, signatures, seal), record specific facts (names, dates, claims) and remain easy to file, print and archive. The discipline is restraint — hierarchy, proportion and consistency carry the formality, not decoration.

## 2. Why does it matter?

A certificate is a permanent artefact: recipients frame it, employers file it, and its typography encodes how official the credential feels. The most important line on it — the recipient's name — is routinely the worst-set element in amateur work (too small, wrong font, squeezed between ornaments). Documents also operate as systems: a letterhead, a memo template, a certificate and a report cover share one institutional identity, and inconsistency between them erodes the credibility the certificate is trying to establish. For event organisations, certificates are often the last branded item a participant receives, so they extend the identity governed in [Visual identity](../branding/visual-identity.md).

Formal documents also carry legal and archival weight that decorative formats do not: the credential statement is a claim the institution stands behind, signatures identify accountable people, and the file may be re-read years later by employers, registry bodies or auditors. That is why this page treats the *facts* (names, dates, statements) as design material of the highest rank — the discipline is closer to typesetting law than to composing an advertisement, and the evaluation checklist reflects it.

## 3. Core principles

- **Formal hierarchy has a fixed apex: the recipient's name.** Everything else — title of the certificate, institution, date, signatures — is scaffolding around that name.
- **Authority comes from structure, not ornament.** A disciplined grid, generous margins, two typefaces and one accent colour read as official; clip-art borders and five fonts read as fake.
- **Facts are unambiguous and machine-checkable.** Full names, precise dates, the exact credential statement, and (for verifiable credentials) an ID or verification URL.
- **Consistency across the document family.** Letterhead, memo, certificate and cover pages share grid, margins, logo zone and footer rules — the same template-system logic as [Posters](posters.md) and [Social media graphics](social-media.md).
- **Accessibility applies to documents too.** Reading order, contrast, real text (not images of text) and adequate type size make documents usable for everyone, including screen-reader users and low-vision recipients.
- **The facts outrank the aesthetics.** A certificate's spelling of a name, its explicit date and its precise credential claim are the product; layout serves them. When beauty and accuracy conflict — a long name that does not fit the display face at full size — accuracy wins and the type steps down per a defined fallback rule.
- **Restraint scales.** Two typefaces, one accent colour, one border system cover every formal document an organisation will ever need; every additional ornament multiplies inconsistency risk across the family.

## 4. How it works

### 4.1 Formal hierarchy for certificates

The elements, in reading priority:

| Rank | Element | Typical treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recipient name | The largest line on the page — conventionally 2–3× any other line's size; often the only place for a display or script face |
| 2 | Certificate title | "Certificate of Achievement / Participation / Completion" — set above the name, steady and quiet |
| 3 | Credential statement | One sentence: what was achieved, by whom, on what programme |
| 4 | Date | Explicit day-month-year; a bare year is not a date |
| 5 | Signatories | Name, title, signature — one or two, never a committee |
| 6 | Seal / logo / credential ID | Institutional marks plus, for verifiable credentials, an ID or URL |

A conventional composition: institution header at top (logo centred or top-left per the letterhead system), certificate title ~one quarter down, name dominating the optical centre, credential statement directly beneath, then date, then a signature block across the lower third with the seal balanced to one side. The name's prominence is the point: when the certificate is framed on a wall, the name is what visitors should read from 2–3 metres.

Name-setting specifics:

- Size: as a conventional starting point, the name runs 60–96 pt on an A4 certificate, so it beats every other element decisively.
- Case: set the name as written by its owner — capitalisation, accents, hyphens and multi-part surnames are facts, not styling. Get the spelling from the source data, never retype it.
- Face: a restrained script or high-quality serif suits the ceremonial register; it must still be legible — decorative scripts that blur at print size defeat the purpose (see [Typeface selection](../typography/typeface-selection.md)).

### 4.2 Borders and formal framing

Borders are traditional but optional; when used:

- **Simple rule systems beat ornamental frames.** A single 1–3 pt rule, or a double rule (thick outer, thin inner) set 8–15 mm from the trim, reads formal without clip-art.
- **Keep the border inside the safe area.** If the frame is part of the design, it must sit at least 5 mm inside trim so cutting drift never clips it; full-bleed background tints still need proper bleed mechanics ([Posters](posters.md) §5.2).
- **Corner ornaments, if any, appear once.** Repeating flourishes on all four corners plus a centre seal plus a watermark is the signature of unedited templates.
- **Watermarks and background tints** stay very low contrast so they never interfere with text legibility; verify text-over-tint contrast ([Colour accessibility](../colour/colour-accessibility.md)).

### 4.3 Signatures, seals and verification

- **Signature block anatomy:** signature (image or ink line), the signatory's name set in text, their title/office, and the date if the signature date differs from the credential date. Two signatories max — organiser and, where relevant, an institutional authority.
- Signature images should sit on a short baseline rule or above the printed name; never float signature PNGs without alignment to the name beneath.
- **Seals:** printed seals (foil, emboss or flat print) sit bottom-centre or lower-left/right, balanced against the signature block, partially over the border line only in traditions where that is intended. An embossed seal needs unprinted space behind it — coordinate with the print vendor.
- **Verifiable credentials:** for modern issuance, add a credential ID and verification URL/QR (small, bottom corner, ~15–20 mm if a QR per the print-size guidance in [Posters](posters.md) §5.5). The visual certificate remains readable *without* the QR.

### 4.4 Document consistency: letterheads and memos

The document family shares one architecture:

- **Grid:** identical margins across A4 and US Letter stock (see 4.5); logo locked to the same top-left or top-centre position on every item.
- **Header zone:** logo + organisation name and, optionally, a rule separating header from body.
- **Footer zone:** address, contact, registration/charity numbers, website — set small (8–9 pt) and consistent; this is the zone most often redesigned per document, which is exactly the drift to prevent.
- **Body rules:** 10–11 pt serif or sans for body text, 1.15–1.4 line spacing per [Typesetting](../typography/typesetting.md), left-aligned ragged right.
- **Memos:** memo fields (to/from/date/subject) as a fixed block, then body; same letterhead grid so memos and letters interfile in one archive without looking like different organisations.

### 4.5 Print considerations: paper, margins, binding

- **Stock sizes:** A4 (210 × 297 mm, ISO 216) is the international default; US Letter (8.5 × 11 in / 216 × 279 mm) is the North American standard. Certificates are commonly issued on heavier stock — 160–300 gsm is a conventional range, with 250 gsm+ for framed-quality awards. A-series dimensions verified at [search-verified] papersizes.org; Letter is a North American industry convention.
- **Margins:** ≥ 20 mm on all sides for text; where left-edge binding or filing applies, use an asymmetric margin — a conventional 25 mm left / 15–20 mm right gutter keeps punch holes and staples out of the text block.
- **Print space for seals and stamps** (where physical stamping happens) must be reserved as clear space in the layout, usually lower-right or across the signature area.
- **Export:** print PDF with fonts embedded, at trim size (certificates rarely need bleed unless the background runs to the edge), and 300 PPI raster elements — same resolution mechanics as [Posters](posters.md) §5.3. For archival copies, a tagged, text-preserving PDF serves both accessibility and records.
- **Personalisation at scale:** build one template with variable fields (name, programme, date) driven by mail-merge or scripted generation; never hand-edit names on copies. This guarantees the name facts stay correct — the highest-stakes text on the page.

### 4.6 Accessibility of documents

- **Real text, not images of text.** The name, dates and credential statement must be selectable text; scanned-image certificates are unreadable to assistive technology and unverifiable by search.
- **Reading order.** In PDF exports, tag content so a screen reader meets: title → recipient name → credential statement → date → signatories → institutional footer. A visually beautiful certificate with a jumbled tag order fails for blind recipients.
- **Contrast and size.** Body text ≥ 10–11 pt; text contrast well above the minimum for body copy (WCAG 2 AA requires 4.5:1 for normal text — [canonical]); gold-on-cream and pale-grey-on-white ceremonial palettes routinely fail this and should be darkened or reserved for large display text.
- **Colour-independent meaning.** Nothing essential (e.g. "pass" vs "distinction") may be signalled by colour alone.
- **Tagging in practice.** When exporting the PDF: set document title and language in metadata, verify the reading order in the tagging panel (title → name → statement → date → signatures → footer), add alt text to the seal/signature images, and confirm the file passes a screen-reader smoke test — the name read aloud correctly is the single most important check. Underlying principles (contrast ratios, real text, logical structure) are the same ones the web relies on; see [Colour accessibility](../colour/colour-accessibility.md) for the contrast side.

### 4.7 Multi-page documents: programmes and booklets

Event programmes, reports and booklets extend the same system across spreads:

- **Page setup:** design in spreads (two pages facing) but supply single pages unless the printer binds saddle-stitch; a saddle-stitched A4 booklet is built from A3 spreads with total page counts in multiples of four.
- **Folio and running elements:** page numbers, running headers/footers and section labels are master-page elements — never typed per page. Folios sit outside the text block (bottom corners or outer edges) so they survive binding trimming.
- **Margins grow at the spine:** add 3–5 mm to the bound edge so text never disappears into the gutter — the asymmetric-margin logic of §4.5 applied to every page.
- **Hierarchy repeats per page:** section openers reuse the certificate's title logic (one large element, generous whitespace); content pages reuse the letterhead grid.
- **Consistency is the point:** a booklet is the document family at scale — same grid, same footer, same two typefaces; nothing new is invented mid-document.

### 4.8 Credential language and register

The credential statement is a formula, and its wording should be settled before layout:

- **Participation:** "This certifies that [Name] participated in [Programme] on [Date]." Honest, minimal; it claims attendance, not achievement.
- **Completion:** "…has successfully completed [Programme], comprising [hours/modules]" — the claim grows, so the evidence named in the statement must back it.
- **Achievement/award:** "…is awarded [Distinction] for [specific accomplishment]" — the *specific* accomplishment is what makes the document meaningful years later.
- Register rules: full institutional voice, no abbreviations in the statement, no marketing adjectives ("amazing", "incredible") — formality is the register that survives framing on a wall. The certificate title and statement are fixed strings in the template; only bracketed variables merge from data.

### 4.9 Metadata, naming and archival

- Set PDF metadata deliberately: document title (not the file name), author/organisation, language. Screen readers and document systems rely on it, and an untitled PDF window looks careless in professional circulation.
- Name generated files deterministically: `certificate_2027-03-14_doe-jane.pdf` — recipient, date, template version — so a merge run of 400 files remains auditable.
- Archive the master template (with its font files and licence notes) alongside the outputs; next quarter's event should re-open a working master, not reconstruct one.

## 5. Practical application

A production sequence for an event certificate run:

1. **Collect facts from source data:** exact names, programme titles, dates, signatory names/titles. Names are verified against registration data, never retyped from memory.
2. **Build the template** on the letterhead grid: border system, header, name zone (largest type), credential statement slot, date, signature block, seal zone, credential ID slot.
3. **Set the type system:** two faces maximum — one ceremonial face for the certificate title and name, one workhorse for everything else (see [Font pairing](../typography/font-pairing.md)).
4. **Merge variables and proof 10% of the run** at 100% print scale: check longest name fits the zone (provide a smaller-size fallback rule for very long names rather than shrinking the font ad hoc per certificate), accents render, dates correct.

### 5.1 Data hygiene for personalisation

The merge is where certificate projects actually fail, so treat the data as part of the design:

- **Pull names from the source of truth** (registration data) with explicit fields for given name and family name — never a single "name" string typed by registrants in any case they liked.
- **Normalise casing programmatically**, then manually review exceptions: Mc/Mac/O' prefixes, multi-part surnames, particles (van, de, bin), and mixed-script names keep their registrant-approved capitalisation rather than a title-case algorithm's guess.
- **Verify Unicode**: accents, diacritics and non-Latin scripts must survive export — embed fonts that cover the actual name set, and test-print the alphabetically extreme names of the run.
- **Deduplicate and version** the recipient list; every re-issue gets a new file version, never an overwrite, so an archived run remains auditable.
- **Fallback rule for long names:** reduce from 84 pt to 72 pt, then 60 pt, in fixed steps — a rule in the template, applied by the merge, so no certificate is hand-tweaked.
5. **Export** embedded-font PDFs named by recipient; archive the master template for the next event.

## 6. Decision framework

- **Certificate, badge or digital credential?** Ceremonial/permanent recognition → printed certificate. On-the-day identity → badge ([Event collateral](event-collateral.md)). Verifiable, shareable proof → digital credential with an ID/URL; the three are complements, not rivals.
- **One or two signatories?** Default one (the organiser). Add a second only when external authority genuinely matters (a partner institution, a verifying body).
- **Border or clean sheet?** Formal institutional traditions (diplomas, awards) → restrained border. Modern corporate contexts → clean sheet with a header rule; both are legitimate registers — pick one per institution and keep it.
- **Colour paper or white?** White or near-white stock keeps contrast and legibility; tinted stock only where the brand palette requires it, and then re-check text contrast.
- **Print process and quantity.** Handfuls → office laser on heavy stock (fast, fine for flat designs); formal runs → digital press with proofs (metallic foils and embossing need specialist vendors and extra lead time — book it with the event timeline, not the print timeline).
- **Single certificate or a document family?** If the organisation issues letters and memos at all, build the letterhead grid first (§4.4) and derive the certificate from it; a one-off certificate designed in isolation is the first step towards a fragmented identity.
- **Landscape or portrait?** Portrait for filing and framing conventions; landscape where the layout genuinely benefits (wide signature blocks, medal imagery) — 7.3 shows the tier logic for choosing.
- **Digital issuance alongside print?** If recipients will share the credential online, issue a matching digital PDF (tagged, verifiable ID) from the same template — the print artefact and the digital artefact must not diverge in wording or identity. The verification decision is one line in the template: ID only, ID + URL, or ID + QR (§4.3), chosen by how the organisation actually checks credentials.
- **How formal is the register?** Match the organisation's existing correspondence; a community event's certificate can be warmer in the statement's phrasing while keeping the formal structure — register lives in word choice (§4.8), not in broken hierarchy.

## 7. Examples

### 7.1 Event participation certificate spec (A4 portrait)

```text
Stock:      A4 210 × 297 mm, 250 gsm, portrait
Margins:    20 mm; double-rule border (1.5 pt outer, 0.5 pt inner) at 10 mm from trim
Header:     organisation logo 18 mm high, top-centre at 15 mm from top rule
Title:      "Certificate of Participation", 28 pt, ceremonial face, small caps
Name:       84 pt, ceremonial face, optical centre; accent rule 40 mm beneath
Statement:  16 pt, one sentence naming programme and dates
Date:       14 pt, explicit day-month-year
Signatures: 2 blocks lower-third — signature image, name 13 pt, title 10 pt,
            short rule above each; printed seal 30 mm lower-left
ID:         credential ID 8 pt + 18 mm QR bottom-right
Ink:        dark charcoal text; single accent from palette for rules and seal
```

### 7.2 Letterhead system (A4 and Letter)

One grid: logo top-left 15 mm high at 20 mm margins; organisation wordmark beside it; footer 8 pt with address, contacts and registration number above a 0.5 pt rule; body zone starts 45 mm from top. The same grid produces the memo template (to/from/date/subject block at the top of the body zone) and the certificate above — one family, one archive.

### 7.3 Merit award variant (A4 landscape)

The award variant differentiates rank from participation while staying in the same system: landscape orientation and a warmer stock (300 gsm) signal the higher tier; the name moves to 96 pt with the ceremonial face; the credential statement names the achievement ("awarded to ... for first place in the Design Sprint"); a foil seal replaces the printed one. Tier logic mirrors the sponsor matrix in [Event collateral](event-collateral.md): rank differences are expressed through size, stock and seal — never through a different layout that breaks the family resemblance.

### 7.4 Memo template spec

```text
Grid:      letterhead grid unchanged (logo top-left, footer rule)
Fields:    TO / FROM / DATE / SUBJECT block, labels 10 pt bold caps,
           values 11 pt regular, subject line carries the summary sentence
Body:      11 pt, 1.3 line spacing, left-aligned; ≤ 1 page by convention
Footer:    unchanged letterhead footer — memos must interfile with letters
Rationale: a memo that redesigns the letterhead breaks the archive's unity
```

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Name too small.** The recipient's name set smaller than the certificate title. Correction: make the name the largest line by a factor of 2–3.
- **Misspelled or wrongly capitalised names.** Hand-typed names drift from source data. Correction: merge from registration data; proof every name, including accents and hyphens.
- **Clip-art borders and ornament overload.** Four corners of flourishes plus watermark plus seal. Correction: one restrained rule system, one seal.
- **Signature chaos.** Floating signature images, three signatories, titles missing. Correction: signature above a rule, name and title beneath, two signatories maximum.
- **Missing or vague dates.** A bare year, or no date at all. Correction: explicit day-month-year.
- **Unembedded fonts in the PDF.** The ceremonial face substitutes on another machine and the layout collapses. Correction: embed or outline fonts at export.
- **Contrast failures in ceremonial palettes.** Gold on cream, pale grey on white. Correction: darken inks; reserve metallics for foil accents, not body text.
- **Image-only certificates.** Text baked into a JPEG: unreadable, unsearchable, unverifiable. Correction: live text in a tagged PDF.
- **Per-document redesign.** Each letterhead and memo drifting in margins and logo position. Correction: one document system from a shared master (see §4.4).
- **Credential over-claiming.** "Certificate of Excellence" for attendance. Correction: match the statement formula to what actually happened (§4.8); inflated claims damage the institution, not just the document.
- **Merge-field disasters.** `<<First_Name>>` printed on 200 certificates, or title-cased "Mcdonald" and "o'brien". Correction: the data-hygiene pipeline in §5.1 plus a 10% human proof.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- [ ] Recipient name is the largest element (≈2–3× any other line) and reads clearly from 2–3 m.
- [ ] Names verified against source data: spelling, accents, capitalisation, long-name fallback rule.
- [ ] Certificate title, credential statement, explicit date, signatory names and titles all present.
- [ ] Border (if any) sits inside the safe area; background tints run to bleed only where intended.
- [ ] Signature blocks aligned: signature, name, title, rule; at most two signatories.
- [ ] Seal placed with reserved clear space; QR (if used) ≥ 15–20 mm with quiet zone and a plain-text ID beside it.
- [ ] Document family shares grid, logo zone and footer across letterhead, memo and certificate.
- [ ] Export: fonts embedded, ≥ 300 PPI raster elements, tagged PDF with correct reading order.
- [ ] Body text ≥ 10–11 pt; contrast passes WCAG 2 AA minimums (4.5:1 body text).
- [ ] A 10% proof at 100% scale shows no clipped names, broken accents or substituted fonts.
- [ ] Merge data passed the hygiene pipeline: source-of-truth fields, casing exceptions reviewed, Unicode verified, fallback rule applied (§5.1).
- [ ] Credential statement matches the claim actually made (participation vs completion vs award) per §4.8.
- [ ] PDF metadata set (title, author, language) and files named deterministically for the archive (§4.9).
