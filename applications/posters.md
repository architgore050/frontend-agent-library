# Posters

## 1. What is it?

A poster is a single-sheet graphic designed to communicate one message to people who are walking past it. Unlike a document, it is never read linearly and never "finished" — it is sampled in seconds. A poster must therefore deliver its core message (usually: what is happening, when, where, and what the viewer should do about it) through hierarchy, scale and contrast rather than through prose. Posters exist in two physical regimes that demand different discipline: **hand-held formats** (A4–A3, read at arm's length) and **wall-mounted formats** (A2 and up, read at 1–10 metres). The same design logic applies, but every numeric decision — type size, stroke weight, image resolution — changes with viewing distance.

## 2. Why does it matter?

The poster is the most compressed test of a designer's hierarchy skills: every competing element is on one sheet, and the viewer's attention is a scarce, passing resource. A weak poster fails silently — nobody complains, the room simply ignores it. For an agent producing event or campaign collateral, the poster is usually the first artefact that extends a fixed logo into a full composition, so the decisions made here (type scale, colour roles, grid, image treatment) typically cascade into banners, social graphics and badges. Getting the print mechanics right (bleed, resolution, colour space) is also what separates a file that prints cleanly from one that comes back with white slivers at the edges or muddy, pixelated imagery.

Posters also differ from neighbouring formats in *commitment*: a social post can be replaced tomorrow, but a poster printed on stock and posted in venues is expensive to revise and lives for weeks. That asymmetry pushes the poster workflow towards slower verification (the tests in §4.5, a hard proof, a vendor conversation) and away from the rapid-iteration habits of digital channels. When a poster must be updated frequently — weekly programmes, rotating schedules — design it as a template with variable zones from the start, exactly as recurring social content is templatised in [Social media graphics](social-media.md).

## 3. Core principles

- **One focal point.** Exactly one element dominates — either the headline or a single hero image, never both at equal weight. The eye must land somewhere first, and that landing point must be the element that carries the message.
- **Hierarchy in priority order.** A poster answers questions in a fixed sequence: *What is this? → When? → Where? → What should I do?* Visual weight should follow that order: event name > date/time > venue/location > call to action > supporting detail (organisers, sponsors, pricing).
- **Typography at distance.** Type is sized for the worst-case viewer — the person farthest away who still needs the headline. The rule of thumb used in signage applies: roughly 1 inch (25 mm) of capital letter height per 10 feet (3 m) of viewing distance for maximum legibility, with comfortable reading happening at 30–50% closer. ([accessed] Pannier Graphics letter-height chart.)
- **Radical text reduction.** A poster is not a flyer. Body copy beyond one or two short lines almost never gets read; anything that cannot survive being cut does not belong on the sheet. Details (programmes, bios, terms) move to a QR code or URL.
- **Print mechanics are non-negotiable.** Bleed, safe areas, resolution and colour space are production requirements, not styling choices. A beautiful design exported without bleed becomes a defective print run.

## 4. How it works

### 4.1 The information priority stack

Before composing anything, list every piece of content and rank it. A typical event poster collapses to five tiers:

| Tier | Content | Typical visual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Event name / primary claim | Largest type or integrated into the hero image; 1–3 second read |
| 2 | Date and time | Distinct weight or colour, never buried in a sentence |
| 3 | Location / venue | Own line, own size; include city for out-of-town audiences |
| 4 | Call to action | Register / buy / attend free — one action, stated as a verb |
| 5 | Supporting detail | Organiser, sponsors, website, QR code, accessibility notes |

Tier 5 may include sponsor logos, but sponsor lockups must never out-rank tiers 1–3; see [Brand architecture](../branding/brand-architecture.md) for sub-brand and sponsor lockup rules.

### 4.2 Focal point strategy

There are three workable focal strategies, and mixing them is the classic beginner failure:

1. **Image-led.** A photograph or illustration carries the emotional message; the headline is large but secondary in visual mass. Works for atmosphere-driven events (concerts, exhibitions, community days).
2. **Type-led.** The headline *is* the image — expressed through extreme scale, weight or a custom arrangement. Works for conferences, talks and sales where the words are the attraction.
3. **Composition-led.** An abstract focal device (a shape, a colour field, a repeated motif from the brand) pulls the eye, with type doing the informational work. Common in cultural and institutional posters.

Whichever is chosen, everything else is subordinated through the contrast principles in [Principles of design](../foundations/principles-of-design.md): the focal element should win on at least two axes (scale, colour, weight, isolation) while secondary elements win on none.

### 4.3 Typography at distance

The signage rule — about 25 mm of capital height per 3 m of viewing distance — converts into poster guidance once you note that capital height is roughly 70% of the nominal point size. The table below gives conventional starting points (derived from that rule, not hard law; verify against your venue):

| Format | Typical reading distance | Headline (cap height ≈) | Body / detail minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| A4 / letter (hand-held) | 0.3–0.5 m | 30–48 pt | 10–12 pt |
| A3 | 0.5–1 m | 48–72 pt | 12–14 pt |
| A2 | 1–2 m | 72–120 pt | 14–18 pt |
| A1 | 2–4 m | 120–200 pt | 18–24 pt |
| A0 | 4–8 m | 200–340 pt | 24 pt and up |

Additional distance rules that matter more than raw size:

- **Sans serif and open letterforms win.** As viewing distance grows, thin strokes, serifs and condensed or decorative faces lose legibility first; save them for accent words only. See [Typeface selection](../typography/typeface-selection.md).
- **Contrast extends legibility; weak contrast can halve the readable distance** ([accessed] Pannier). Dark-on-light or light-on-dark with clear value separation, verified with the checks in [Colour accessibility](../colour/colour-accessibility.md).
- **Mixed case reads faster than all-caps** for anything longer than a few words; all-caps is acceptable for short headlines if tracking is opened slightly.
- **Line length discipline:** 3–7 words per headline line; body blocks, if any, left-aligned and ragged-right per [Typesetting](../typography/typesetting.md).

### 4.4 Grid and composition

Even a "freeform" poster benefits from an underlying grid: it forces margins, aligns tiers, and makes series (same event, different dates) trivially consistent. Use the column logic in [Grid systems](../layout/grid-systems.md) and the proportional spacing in [Spacing and proportion](../layout/spacing-and-proportion.md). Two poster-specific conventions:

- **Outer margins scale with sheet size** — roughly 4–5% of the shortest side, so an A3 poster runs a 12–15 mm margin and an A0 poster 40–50 mm. Text never touches the margin.
- **Anchor corners deliberately.** One strong corner (usually bottom-left or bottom-right) assembles the "small print" tier (venue, date block, CTA, QR) so the eye has a predictable exit point.

### 4.5 Testing hierarchy before print

Three fast, repeatable tests catch most poster failures before money is spent:

1. **Three-second test.** Show the poster to someone for three seconds, then ask: what is the event, when is it, where is it? Every unanswered question is a hierarchy defect. Run it on colleagues who have not seen the file — familiarity makes designers hallucinate legibility.
2. **Squint/blur test.** Blur your eyes or view the file at ~10–15% zoom. The headline should remain a dominant shape; the date block should still resolve as the second-strongest mass. If the sponsor logo out-masses the headline at blur, demote it.
3. **Distance walk.** Print A4 at full scale (or project the file), step back the realistic reading distance from the table in 4.3, and try to read each tier in order. This validates type sizes and contrast against real eyes rather than the design monitor.

Fix hierarchy defects by changing *relative weight* (size, colour, isolation), not by adding boxes, rules or drop shadows around weak elements — decoration that props up a weak hierarchy usually makes the competition worse, per the emphasis logic in [Principles of design](../foundations/principles-of-design.md).

## 5. Practical application

### 5.1 Print dimensions

The ISO A-series is the default in most of the world; the North American sheet sizes below are conventional trade sizes.

| Size | Dimensions | Pixels at 300 PPI (trim) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| A4 | 210 × 297 mm | 2480 × 3508 | Hand-outs, noticeboards |
| A3 | 297 × 420 mm | 3508 × 4961 | Indoor posters, window displays |
| A2 | 420 × 594 mm | 4961 × 7016 | Corridor and event posters |
| A1 | 594 × 841 mm | 7016 × 9933 | Large indoor, retail windows |
| A0 | 841 × 1189 mm | 9933 × 14043 | Conference halls, hoardings |
| 11 × 17 in | 279 × 432 mm | 3300 × 5100 | US tabloid poster |
| 18 × 24 in | 457 × 610 mm | 5400 × 7200 | US street/small venue |
| 24 × 36 in | 610 × 914 mm | 7200 × 10800 | US large venue, bus shelters |

The A-series (ISO 216) halves each sheet into the next size down (A3 = A4 × 2), which makes one design scale cleanly across a whole campaign ([search-verified] papersizes.org). The B series (B1 = 707 × 1000 mm) sits between A sizes for jobs that need slightly larger sheets ([canonical] ISO 216).

### 5.2 Bleed, trim and safe areas

Three concentric zones govern every print file:

```text
┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│  BLEED: artwork extends here    │
│  ┌───────────────────────────┐  │
│  │  TRIM: where the cutter   │  │
│  │  aims (final size)        │  │
│  │  ┌─────────────────────┐  │  │
│  │  │ SAFE: keep text and │  │  │
│  │  │ logos inside here   │  │  │
│  │  └─────────────────────┘  │  │
│  └───────────────────────────┘  │
└─────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Zone | Standard | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Bleed | 3 mm per edge (ISO/EU) or 0.125 in / 3.2 mm (US) | Absorbs cutting tolerance; backgrounds and images must run to the bleed edge ([search-verified] Polotno bleed calculator) |
| Trim | Final dimensions (e.g. A3 = 297 × 420 mm) | The delivered size |
| Safe area / margin | ≥ 3–5 mm inside trim (more on larger sheets) | No text, logos, QR codes or faces cross it — cutting drift would clip them |

At 300 PPI, 3 mm of bleed adds roughly 35 pixels per edge. An A1 poster with bleed therefore exports at about 7087 × 10004 px. For very large sheets (A0 and up) some large-format shops accept 100–150 PPI because viewing distance is measured in metres; confirm with the printer before downgrading.

### 5.3 Resolution and raster quality

- **300 PPI at final trim size is the print standard** for close-viewed print ([search-verified] Polotno bleed calculator and 4over4 print guidance). Design at final size; do not plan to upscale.
- Raster images pulled from the web are usually 72–150 PPI at screen size and will not survive enlargement — source images at, or larger than, the target pixel dimensions.
- Vector elements (logos, type, rules, flat shapes) are resolution-independent and should stay vector through to the print PDF; see [Logo systems](../branding/logo-systems.md) for artwork format discipline.
- A quick self-check: view the exported file at 100% zoom at arm's length. Anything soft at A4 will be visibly soft at A1.

### 5.4 Colour space for print

- Print reproduces with cyan/magenta/yellow/black (CMYK) inks, not light; a screen-first RGB design must be converted, and some RGB colours (vivid greens, electric blues, saturated orange) will shift or dull. The mechanics of colour values and conversion live in [Colour in code](../colour/colour-in-code.md); palette construction in [Palette development](../colour/palette-development.md).
- **Black text and thin black rules use 100% K only.** A four-colour "rich black" on small type registers poorly and looks fuzzy.
- Large solid dark areas (a full navy background, for example) usually *should* be a rich black (a CMYK build — e.g. C60 M40 Y40 K100, a conventional starting point) to avoid printing grey.
- Ink coverage is capped by the printer (commonly around 300% total ink coverage) — ask the vendor; heavy builds above the cap cause drying and offsetting problems on uncoated stock.
- If the poster will be printed digitally on a large-format inkjet (the common case for events), RGB files are often acceptable or even preferred; confirm the vendor's pipeline. What never changes: pick one space, colour-manage consistently, and proof critical brand colours.

### 5.5 QR codes and URLs

- **Print size, not pixel size, governs scannability.** A widely used rule of thumb: the QR's printed width should be at least one-tenth of the expected scanning distance (scan at 1 m → ≥ 100 mm wide; scan at arm's length 30 cm → ≥ 30 mm). Treat this as a conventional starting point — test on the intended stock at real size.
- Supply the QR as vector artwork with its quiet zone (the empty margin around the code, typically four modules wide) preserved; do not let a texture or gradient bleed into it.
- Always pair the QR with a short human-readable URL and the CTA verb ("Register at short.url/xyz") — some viewers will not scan, and print QRs cannot be clicked.
- Use error correction level M or Q so the code survives scuffs; point it at a short, trackable link rather than a 200-character URL (shorter URLs produce simpler, denser-resistant codes).

### 5.6 Stock, finishing and durability

Paper choice changes both perception and survival of a poster. Conventional trade ranges — confirm availability locally:

| Stock / finish | Weight | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matte coated | 130–170 gsm | Indoor event posters | Flattens glare; text-heavy designs read best |
| Gloss coated | 130–200 gsm | Photographic, retail | Makes images pop; glare under venue lights |
| Uncoated | 120–250 gsm | Cultural, artisanal register | Inks absorb and darken slightly; proof colours |
| Blue-back / wildposting | 90–115 gsm | Street posting runs | Opaque backing hides the sheet beneath |
| Synthetic / waterproof | 120–200 µm | Outdoor, wet weather | Check ink adhesion; often needs UV or latex printing |
| Lamination (matte/gloss) | +30–125 µm | High-traffic, reusable | Matte for under-lights legibility (glare kills reading) |

Durability is a function of light and touch: UV fade affects vivid magentas and yellows first on windows; laminate or synthetic stock solves handling wear on noticeboards. Screen posters are covered in 5.7.

### 5.7 Digital poster surfaces

Screens in venues (TVs, totems, video walls) increasingly replace printed posters. Different mechanics apply:

- **Design in pixels for the panel's native resolution** (a 1080p screen is a 1920 × 1080 canvas), RGB, no bleed concept — the safe area replaces it: keep content inside roughly 90% of the panel because overscan and bezels clip edges.
- **Legibility shifts with ambience:** screen contrast is high but small text flickers and reflection washes dark text; use the same distance tables but lean towards the comfortable-distance sizes.
- **Rotation and dwell:** a poster on screen may show for 5–10 seconds in a loop; the core message must survive that fragment, so front-load event name/date and consider a static "hold" frame with fuller detail.
- **Motion with restraint:** subtle loops draw the eye; full-screen animation reduces legibility and annoys passers-by who are mid-read.

### 5.8 Export checklist for print

1. Canvas = trim size + bleed on all sides.
2. All text and key graphics inside the safe area.
3. Images ≥ 300 PPI at final size (or the vendor's accepted lower figure for large format).
4. Colour space agreed with the vendor; rich black on large solids, 100% K on text.
5. Fonts embedded or outlined in the final PDF.
6. Flatten transparency where the vendor requires it; export PDF/X-compatible if asked.

### 5.9 Working with the print vendor

The vendor conversation is part of the design job, and it happens before artwork is finished, not after:

- **Ask for the spec sheet first:** accepted file format (PDF/X variants preferred for offset), colour profile to use, bleed requirement, resolution floor, and whether they want scaled files for large format. Every parameter in §5.2–§5.4 should come from this sheet, not from habit.
- **Order a hard-copy proof** for colour-critical work: screen colour is a promise the press may not keep, and the difference between a calibrated proof and a monitor is where brand colours die.
- **Agree the paper before designing:** stock affects ink behaviour (uncoated darkens, coated saturates), and late stock changes silently invalidate colour decisions.
- **Batch deadline-driven work:** posters rarely travel alone; asking one vendor for posters, badges and boards in one quoted job usually buys consistency and a better rate than three separate rush orders.
- Keep the vendor's final spec sheet with the template archive — the next event starts with answers, not with questions.

## 6. Decision framework

- **Choose size from the venue, not the ambition.** Hand-held (A4–A3) for personal distribution and noticeboards; A2–A1 for corridors, shops and exhibition walls where viewers stop; A0 or larger only for spaces read from across a room, where the message must shrink to a name, a date and one image.
- **Choose focal strategy from the content.** If the event's appeal is atmosphere, go image-led; if the appeal is who is speaking or what is promised, go type-led; if the brand identity itself is strong, a composition-led system poster may carry the series.
- **Choose print process by quantity and deadline.** Digital (short runs, fast, per-copy cost flat) vs offset (economical above roughly 500–1000 copies, needs colour-accurate proofs) — a conventional industry breakpoint, confirm locally.
- **One message per sheet.** If two messages compete (e.g. the event *and* a separate sponsor campaign), they belong on separate posters or in a governed hierarchy with the sponsor clearly subordinate — see [Brand architecture](../branding/brand-architecture.md).
- **Choose stock from context.** Indoor short-run → matte coated 130–170 gsm; photographic window work → gloss; outdoor → synthetic or laminated (§5.6 table). The stock decision affects colour proofing: uncoated darkens inks, so proof on the real substrate.
- **Choose print process by quantity and deadline.** Digital (short runs, fast, per-copy cost flat) vs offset (economical above roughly 500–1000 copies, needs colour-accurate proofs) — a conventional industry breakpoint, confirm locally.
- **Printed, screen, or both?** If the venue has screens, design the digital variant as a native pixel file (§5.7) rather than exporting the print PDF to a screen, which crops to the wrong ratio and shows bezel-cut content.
- **Series or single?** More than three posters for one event → build the series grid and template first (7.2); singles can be art-directed freely.

## 7. Examples

### 7.1 Event poster spec (A2, image-led)

```text
Canvas:      420 × 594 mm + 3 mm bleed  (4961 × 7016 px @ 300 PPI + bleed)
Grid:        12 columns, 15 mm outer margin
Tier 1:      Event name, 160 pt, custom lockup position over image, top third
Tier 2:      Date + time, 36 pt semibold, one line
Tier 3:      Venue + city, 28 pt, anchored bottom-left corner
Tier 4:      CTA "Register free", 32 pt on solid brand-colour bar
Tier 5:      Organiser logo 20 mm, QR 40 mm, supporting URL 12 pt
Colour:      CMYK per palette; text 100% K; rich black C60 M40 Y40 K100
Typography:  Sans serif throughout; headline tracking +10
```

### 7.2 Conference poster series (A1, type-led, three sessions)

One master grid and type system; per-session variables are a session number, three colours swapped from the palette, and one speaker photograph cropped identically. Because A-series sheets share proportions, the A1 master re-exports at A3 for noticeboards with only margin adjustments. This is the template-system thinking used across all collateral (see [Event collateral](event-collateral.md)).

### 7.3 Sale poster (A3, type-led)

```text
Canvas:      297 × 420 mm + 3 mm bleed (3508 × 4961 px @ 300 PPI + bleed)
Tier 1:      "-40%" set as the focal element at ~200 pt, accent colour, upper half
Tier 2:      "Two days only · 14–15 March" 40 pt, directly beneath
Tier 3:      Store + district 24 pt; opening hours 16 pt
Tier 4:      "Shop early for best selection" 18 pt italic — expectation-setting CTA
Grid:        Single column, generous isolation around Tier 1; no image at all
Colour:      Two inks: black + one palette accent; discount in accent, rest in 100% K
Rationale:   The offer is the image; every other element serves the number
```

### 7.4 Digital poster variant (venue screen)

The same campaign re-authored for a 1920 × 1080 lobby screen: event name and date occupy the middle band at distance-adjusted sizes, a subtle 6-second loop holds 4 seconds of static "read frame", and the QR/URL moves to a persistent bottom-right corner inside the 90% safe area. Pixel dimensions replace print mechanics entirely — no bleed, RGB, panel-native canvas (§5.7).

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Wall of text.** Programme details, speaker bios or paragraph-length descriptions on the sheet. Correction: cut to the priority stack; move detail behind a QR/URL.
- **Weak or missing hierarchy.** Date buried in a sentence like "the event happens on Saturday 14 March" instead of standing as its own element. Correction: give every tier its own line, size and position.
- **Focal point competition.** Big image *and* big headline *and* bright logo all fighting; the eye never lands. Correction: demote two of the three.
- **Unreadable contact information.** A 10 pt phone number or full street address at A2 scale. Correction: keep one action and one URL/QR; nobody calls a poster.
- **No bleed or content in bleed.** White hairlines at the edges after trimming. Correction: rebuild the canvas with bleed; extend backgrounds past trim.
- **Pixelated imagery.** Screenshot or web images upscaled to poster size. Correction: resourcing, not sharpening — obtain higher-resolution originals or redesign image-led elements as vector.
- **RGB-optimised colours sent to CMYK press.** Neon greens and blues print dull. Correction: soft-proof in CMYK and adjust the palette before export.
- **Ornamental or condensed headline type at distance.** Illegible beyond a few metres. Correction: switch the headline to a high-legibility sans serif; keep display faces to one accent word.
- **Centring everything.** All elements symmetric and mid-weighted; nothing leads. Correction: apply the grid, anchor one corner, create asymmetry on purpose.
- **Series drift.** The third poster in a series redesigns the grid "for variety", and the campaign stops reading as one campaign. Correction: vary only the designated variables (colour swap, session number, image); the grid and type system are frozen (§7.2).
- **Poster as flyer.** Programmes, menus, terms and conditions crowding the sheet. Correction: the priority stack (§4.1) with detail moved behind a QR/URL.
- **Illegible QR practice.** Codes printed at 15 mm for a 2 m scan distance, or darkened by a background gradient. Correction: the one-tenth-of-scan-distance rule and a clean quiet zone (§5.5).
- **Ignoring the substrate.** A design proofed on coated stock printed on uncoated, or a glossy laminate installed under spotlights. Correction: the stock decision in §5.6 plus a hard-copy proof on the real material (§5.9).

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- [ ] Within 3 seconds at the realistic viewing distance, a viewer can answer: what, when, where, and what to do.
- [ ] Exactly one element dominates; secondary tiers are visibly subordinated.
- [ ] Headline capital height matches (or exceeds) the distance table in 4.3.
- [ ] All text and logos sit inside the safe area; backgrounds extend into full bleed.
- [ ] Export is 300 PPI at final size (or vendor-approved lower resolution for large format); fonts embedded.
- [ ] Colour space agreed with the print vendor; body text is 100% K; large solids use a sane rich-black build.
- [ ] QR prints at ≥ one-tenth of scan distance, has its quiet zone, and is paired with a short URL.
- [ ] Sponsor and partner marks do not outrank the primary message.
- [ ] The design survives the squint test — blur your eyes: the hierarchy still reads.
- [ ] The three-second and distance-walk tests (§4.5) were run on eyes that had not seen the file before.
- [ ] Stock and finishing were chosen for the venue context, and colour was proofed on the actual material (§5.6, §5.9).
- [ ] The poster runs through the shared checklist in [Poster checklist](../evaluation/poster-checklist.md) with no unresolved items.
