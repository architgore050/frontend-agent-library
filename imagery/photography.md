# Photography

## 1. What is it?

Photography, in a brand context, is not a collection of nice pictures — it is a component of the visual identity, on the same level as the logo, palette and typography. Every time the brand shows a photograph, it makes a claim about its personality, its honesty and its standard of quality. A photography style is therefore a written set of repeatable decisions: what subjects appear, how close the camera stands, what kind of light is used, how colour is graded, how images are cropped and treated. Defined well, it lets any photographer, stock buyer or image generator produce work that reads as unmistakably "ours".

The vocabulary of that style borrows from the craft of photography: subject selection, composition, lighting, colour grading, treatment and cropping. Each axis can be described in ordinary language without technical training — "people doing real work in daylight, shot from slightly below, warm light, natural colour, generous negative space on the left" is a complete, usable art direction. [Art direction](../graphic-design/art-direction.md) is the broader discipline of steering visual production; this file covers its photographic corner.

This file defines photography styles and the craft behind them. Choosing which images to source, buying stock and deciding whether an image belongs at all is covered in [Image selection](image-selection.md); post-processing techniques, file formats and web delivery live in [Image treatment](image-treatment.md); generating photographs synthetically is covered in [AI image generation](ai-image-generation.md); and the licences and releases that govern what may be used at all are in [Image rights](image-rights.md). Together the five files form the imagery domain: style, selection, treatment, synthesis and clearance.

## 2. Why does it matter?

Photographs usually occupy more pixels on a page or poster than every other brand element combined, and humans read them first. A viewer forms an impression of mood, credibility and social context from an image before reading a single word. This makes photography the most emotionally efficient carrier of brand personality — and the easiest place for an identity to fall apart.

Inconsistency is the characteristic failure. A website that mixes a sunlit documentary shot, a grey studio portrait, a heavily-filtered stock image and a CGI render reads as disorganised even when each individual picture is good. Viewers rarely name the problem ("the white balance differs"), they simply feel that something is off. Roland Barthes' classic analysis in *Rhetoric of the Image* (1964) explains why: a photograph denotes (shows a thing) while also carrying connotations (a mood, a class of setting, an attitude), and readers pick up the connotation instantly and involuntarily. [canonical]

Consistency compounds. When every image shares the same light, distance and grade, the library starts to feel like one authored voice, which builds recall — people begin to recognise the brand's imagery without seeing the logo. That recognition effect is one of the practical goals of defining a style at all.

For the library's reference scenario — extending an already-fixed event logo into a complete brand kit — photography is usually the largest unfinished surface: the mark is settled, but the event's world (its venue, its audience, its energy) exists only as an idea until imagery defines it. A written photography style is what allows that world to be built by several hands — a photographer, a stock purchase, a generated plate — while still arriving as one coherent place. That is why the style is documented before any image is acquired, not reverse-engineered from whatever turned up.

## 3. Core principles

**Coherence beats perfection.** A slightly ordinary photograph that matches the library is worth more than a stunning one that clashes with it. When choosing between two candidates, prefer the one that fits the established light, distance and grade.

**A style is a decision, not a mood.** "Warm and authentic" is a mood. A style is what turns that mood into checkable decisions: natural window light or studio strobe; people or objects; wide environmental scenes or close crop-ins; neutral colour or a warm grade. If a decision cannot be verified by looking at an image, it is not yet part of the style.

**Connotation is the point.** Photographs do not just show subjects, they imply a world. Choose the world deliberately: a hand-built furniture brand connotes craft with sawdust, daylight and imperfect textures; a bank connotes stability with clean architecture, cool neutral light and composed framing. Susan Sontag's *On Photography* (1977) makes the wider argument that photographs are always an interpretation of the world, never a neutral record. [canonical]

**Realness is a dial, not a binary.** Documentary candour (unposed moments, available light) and polished studio control (sculpted light, seamless backdrops) are both legitimate styles; brands live at many points between. The mistake is drifting between the two without noticing, because they signal opposite personalities.

**Describe the outcome, not the equipment.** Art direction works best when it states what the viewer should feel and see. "Shot from slightly below, subject small against a large bright sky, one human figure for scale" is direction a stock search, a photographer and a prompt can all follow.

A note on epistemic status: the framework above — subject, composition, light, colour, treatment — is established professional practice across art direction and photography education (see John Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, 1972; Michael Freeman, *The Photographer's Eye*, 2007, for the craft foundations). [canonical] The specific choices for a given brand are context-dependent recommendations, not laws.

## 4. How it works

A photography style is defined across six axes. For each axis, decide once, write it down, and apply it everywhere.

### 4.1 Subject selection

Subject selection answers "what (and who) does this brand photograph?"

- **People versus objects.** People-centered imagery carries warmth, trust and social proof; object-centred imagery carries precision, control and product focus. Many brands fix a ratio (for example "people appear in at most 40% of images, and never alone — always mid-action") so neither dominates by accident.
- **Activities over possessions.** A photograph of hands working, a conversation happening, or a tool in use communicates more than a static product shot, because the viewer infers a story. Professional convention in editorial and brand work favours "doing" over "posing".
- **Real context versus constructed sets.** Real locations ground the brand in a world; constructed or clean sets communicate polish. Pick one and stay within it.
- **Casting and honesty.** Subjects should look like the audience the brand actually serves. Where images will imply real customers or staff (testimonials, team pages), they must genuinely be those people — see the misinformation discussion in [Image selection](image-selection.md) and the rights consequences of recognisable people in [Image rights](image-rights.md).
- **Objects with a job.** When objects do appear, give them roles: hero objects (the product, the tool) shot as close-up detail accents, and context objects (materials, environments) that set the world. An object with no role is furniture in the frame and a candidate for the omission test in [Image selection](image-selection.md).

### 4.2 Composition

Composition governs framing, distance and angle. The craft foundations (balance, thirds, leading lines, negative space) are covered in [Composition](../foundations/composition.md); the brand layer on top is choosing a *default camera distance* and *default angle* so the library feels shot by one pair of eyes.

- **Distance ladder.** Fix the dominant distance: wide environmental shots (subject small, context rich), medium shots (upper body, activity readable) or close-ups (faces, textures, details). A common professional pattern is one dominant distance plus one accent distance — for example, medium as the workhorse with close-up details as punctuation.
- **Headroom and margins.** Cropping a person needs breathing room around the head, and any framing that may be cropped later needs safe margins. State it: "leave at least 15% empty space on one side of every hero image" is a verifiable rule (conventional starting point, tune to the layout).
- **Angle.** Eye level feels equal and honest; shooting high looks down (small, vulnerable, orderly); shooting low looks up (grand, aspirational). The University of Missouri brand guidelines, for example, direct photographers to "be creative with your angles. Shoot high to evoke grandeur and low for..." [search-verified] — an example of a style expressed as angle instructions.
- **Negative space for function.** Images that will carry overlaid text need planned emptiness. This is a layout-driven constraint, coordinated with [Grid systems](../layout/grid-systems.md): decide in the style guide which side of hero images stays empty.
- **Repeatability over novelty.** Within a library, compositions that repeat — the same angle on successive speakers, the same distance on successive venues — read as intentional. Save the unusual framing for the few images that must punctuate, and let the workhorse composition carry the rest.

### 4.3 Lighting

Lighting determines mood more than any other single variable. Define it in four parts:

- **Source.** Natural (window, overcast sky, golden hour) versus artificial (studio softbox, practical lamps in scene). Natural light connotes authenticity; studio light connotes control and premium finish.
- **Direction.** Front light flattens and flatters; side light sculpts texture and depth; backlight silhouettes and adds atmosphere. Pick the default (professional convention: side or three-quarter light for depth, front light for clean commercial work).
- **Quality and contrast.** Soft light (overcast, diffusion) smooths; hard light (direct sun, bare bulb) dramatises. Direction can be stated as a feeling plus a check ("no harsh black shadows on faces").
- **Colour temperature.** Daylight sits around 5000–6500 K; tungsten interiors run warm around 2700–3500 K (conventional photographic values, not laws). A style that mixes warm interiors and cool daylight will feel inconsistent; fix a temperature family — "always golden-warm" or "always overcast-neutral" — and grade towards it.
- **Consistency across a shoot day.** Light changes hour by hour; a documentary style that embraces it still needs a rule (for example "all exteriors shot in the two hours around sunset") so the day's output lands in one family. Studio styles solve this with fixed setups; natural-light styles solve it with scheduling. Either way, the rule belongs in the written direction, not in the photographer's head.

Real brand examples express lighting as simple, checkable rules. The University of Illinois brand guidelines advise: "Use plenty of natural light, but be cautious when photographing toward windows, as the brightness will make it difficult to capture the subject well" [search-verified]. The University of Missouri directs photography to "incorporate or simulate warm, natural light and sun-drenched settings" [search-verified]. Both define a whole photographic personality with one lighting sentence — that is the level of specificity a brand style needs.

### 4.4 Colour grading

Grading is the post-capture adjustment that pulls every image into the brand's colour world, and it is what most visibly unifies a mixed library. A grade definition should specify, in decreasing importance:

1. **White balance policy** — neutral, or shifted warm/cool by a stated amount. As a conventional starting point on typical editing sliders: a consistent shift of roughly +5 to +15 toward warm on the temperature control, applied identically to every image, is enough to unify without falsifying.
2. **Exposure and contrast policy** — for example "lift shadows slightly, never crush blacks", or "contrasty: strong separation between light and dark" (the pattern a "street level and eye level... strong contrast between light and dark" style description uses, as seen in a photography style summary surfaced during research) [search-verified].
3. **Saturation policy** — natural, muted, or vivid; state whether skin tones are protected from the shift (professional convention: protect skin tones; graded skin that drifts orange or magenta is the most common visible failure).
4. **Signature accents** — optionally, a split-tone (warm highlights, cool shadows) or a slight fade in blacks that becomes the brand's photographic fingerprint.

Mechanics: apply the grade as a saved preset or LUT so it is one click, not a judgment call per image. Grade *after* cropping and retouching, and grade stock images too — grading is the great equaliser between sources, as detailed in [Image treatment](image-treatment.md). It also levels generated imagery: plates arriving from an AI tool with near-but-not-quite brand colour are pulled into tolerance by the same preset, which is why the preset is shared across every acquisition path rather than owned by photographers alone (see [AI image generation](ai-image-generation.md)).

### 4.5 Image treatment

Treatment is the graphic layer applied to photographs — duotones, colour overlays, masks, borders, crops into shapes. Some brands use none (the photograph speaks clean); some make treatment the signature (every image duotoned in brand colours). Both are valid; indecision is not. Treatment recipes, CSS implementations and their accessibility implications are covered in [Image treatment](image-treatment.md). The brand-level decision is simply: is treatment part of our photographic identity, and if so, is it one treatment or a documented family?

### 4.6 Cropping and aspect ratios

Cropping decisions belong in the style guide because they change meaning: a tight crop reads intimate and urgent; a wide crop reads calm and contextual. Define:

- **Master files.** Always retain the original, uncropped, highest-resolution file. Cropping is recomputed per use, never baked into the archive copy.
- **Safe crop zones.** For people, conventional practice keeps eyes in the upper third and protects headroom; state where crops may cut (shoulders, waist) and may not (joints, faces mid-edge).
- **Aspect ratio families.** Fix a small set of ratios and their uses, rather than accepting whatever each platform suggests. As a conventional starting-point table (verify against current platform requirements before a campaign):

| Ratio | Typical use |
| --- | --- |
| 3:2, 4:3 | Editorial and web content images; native to most cameras |
| 16:9 | Web heroes, video thumbnails, presentations |
| 4:5 | Portrait social feed images (taller than square, crops to 1:1 cleanly) |
| 1:1 | Grids, avatars, catalogue tiles |
| 9:16 | Stories, short vertical video, phone-first screens |
| 21:9 or wider | Cinematic banners, ultra-wide heroes |

- **Ratios per context, not per image.** A brand usually needs three families (for example 16:9, 1:1, 4:5) and generates crops of the same master for each — this is what keeps a hero, a grid tile and a story frame feeling like the same photograph. How crops are delivered responsively on the web is covered in [Image treatment](image-treatment.md).
- **Crop for treatment too.** If the style includes duotones or overlays, plan the crop around what survives them: busy, evenly detailed frames turn to mush under heavy treatment; frames with one clear subject and quiet surroundings carry a duotone cleanly. The crop decision and the treatment decision are one conversation, not two departments.

## 5. Practical application

### 5.1 Writing a photography direction one-pager

A usable photography direction fits one page and contains: the mood in one sentence; the six axes as checkable rules; three to five approved reference images ("this, not that" pairs are strongest); and the grade recipe. Writing it in a structured form keeps it portable between humans and tools:

```json
{
  "photography_style": {
    "mood": "energetic, grounded, optimistic",
    "subject": "people mid-action in real workplaces; objects only as part of activity",
    "distance": "medium dominant; close-up details as accents; no distant anonymous crowds",
    "angle": "eye level or slightly low; no top-down table shots",
    "lighting": "natural window/daylight; soft; avoid window-facing backlight",
    "colour_temperature": "warm-neutral (approx. 4800-5300 K equivalent)",
    "grade": {
      "white_balance": "slightly warm (+8 on temperature slider, conventional)",
      "contrast": "moderate; lift shadows, protect highlights",
      "saturation": "natural, skin tones protected",
      "signature": "subtle fade of blacks by 5%"
    },
    "treatment": "none on core photography; duotone reserved for backgrounds",
    "ratios": ["16:9 hero", "4:5 social", "1:1 grid"],
    "negative_space": "left third of hero images kept empty for headline"
  }
}
```

This example is an illustrative conventional template — the values are plausible defaults, not prescriptions. An agent extending an event logo into a full kit can fill this structure from the logo's colour and personality (see [Visual identity](../branding/visual-identity.md) and [Fixed logo analysis](../branding/fixed-logo-analysis.md)), then use it verbatim as a prompt foundation in [AI image generation](ai-image-generation.md).

### 5.2 Working with photographers

When a style is commissioned rather than sourced, the one-pager becomes a brief, and the brief becomes a shot list:

- **Shot list first.** Derive the list from real layout slots (each page or template that needs imagery), not from imagination: "home hero, 16:9, medium-wide, left negative space", "speaker portraits, 4:5, medium, neutral background". Every shot has a slot, an orientation and a purpose before the crew is booked.
- **Anchor images travel.** Attach the approved reference set and the grade recipe; agree that proofs will be delivered graded (or that a grading pass happens in-house) so nothing is judged ungraded.
- **Review in grids.** Review proofs side by side against the anchor set; accept per shot against its slot's requirements, not on general impressiveness.
- **Deliver for the system.** Masters uncropped at full resolution, plus the agreed ratio family per shot; releases collected on site (see [Image rights](image-rights.md)); filenames following the library's conventions.
- **Over-capture deliberately.** Shoot variants (same subject, alternate angle and distance) so crops and future slots have material — reshoots cost far more than extra frames.

### 5.3 Consistency across a library

A photography library stays coherent through process, not memory:

- **One acquisition path where possible.** If commissioning, use one photographer or team per style tier. If buying stock, buy in one batch against a written scorecard (see [Image selection](image-selection.md)) rather than one-off purchases that drift.
- **Grade everything through the same preset.** Imported, commissioned, stock and AI-generated images all pass through the same grading step; this is the single highest-leverage consistency habit.
- **Review in contact sheets.** Evaluate images side by side as a grid, not one by one. Mismatches in light and grade that hide in single view become obvious in a grid.
- **Name and tag for reuse.** Store masters with descriptive filenames and tags (subject, location, orientation, clearance status); an image that cannot be found gets replaced by an off-style substitute.
- **Record clearance at intake.** Every image entering the library carries its rights status from day one — source, licence, release state — so the aesthetic review and the legal review happen on the same file ([Image rights](image-rights.md)).
- **Prune.** Retire images that no longer match the current grade or direction; an out-of-date library pulls the whole system backwards.
- **Assign ownership.** One person (or role) holds the style one-pager and approves deviations; consistency dies in committees and survives with an owner. When the owner changes, the style document — not their memory — carries the style forward.
- **Keep the one-pager where decisions happen.** A style document filed away from the working tools (next to the layout system, in the brand guidelines, beside the prompt templates) gets used; one stored as a PDF nobody opens does not. Link it from wherever imagery is selected or generated.
- **Version the style.** Styles legitimately evolve; record version dates in the one-pager so "the current grade" always means something checkable, and re-grade back-catalogue material when a version lands rather than letting two eras coexist silently.
- **Plan the image budget per template.** Decide how many photographs each layout template actually needs (hero pages may need one strong image; dense utility pages may need none). A budget prevents library bloat and keeps every image in a working slot instead of shelved as "someday" material.

## 6. Decision framework

When defining or reviewing a photography style, resolve the axes in this order, because each constrains the next:

1. **People or objects first** — sets the emotional register and the casting rules.
2. **Real or constructed world** — sets locations, props and casting honesty.
3. **Lighting family** — natural or studio; warm or cool; soft or hard.
4. **Distance and angle** — the camera's habitual position.
5. **Grade** — white balance, contrast, saturation, signature accent.
6. **Treatment and ratios** — the graphic layer and delivery shapes.

Brand personality translates into these axes roughly as follows (context-dependent starting points, not formulas):

| Brand personality | Subject | Distance | Light | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Warm, human, community | People mid-activity, real settings | Medium, eye level | Natural, soft, warm | Slightly warm, natural saturation |
| Precise, premium, technological | Objects and spaces, few people | Wide and close detail mix | Studio or controlled, cool-neutral | Neutral to cool, clean contrast |
| Energetic, youthful, bold | People in motion, tight moments | Close, dynamic angles | Harder light, strong sun | Vivid, strong contrast |
| Calm, cultural, considered | Spaces, materials, solitary figures | Wide, composed | Atmospheric, directional | Muted, gentle contrast |
| Crafted, artisanal, honest | Hands, tools, textures, process | Close detail dominant | Daylight, textured side light | Warm-neutral, visible grain |

For review, ask of any candidate image: does it sit in the right lighting family? Is the camera distance one of the two allowed? Does the grade match the preset output? Is there planned negative space if text will sit on it? Would it be indistinguishable in tone from the rest of the grid? Any "no" is a fixable direction problem — re-shoot, re-crop, re-grade, or reject.

A useful tie-breaker when two images both fit: prefer the one that communicates the message with fewer assumptions (a reader who sees only this one image should still take away the right idea). Decoration without communication is the trap covered in [Image selection](image-selection.md).

When the style is being reviewed rather than defined, invert the process: audit the existing library against the six axes, tally the violations (how many images fall outside the lighting family, outside the distance defaults, outside the grade), and fix the largest class first. Re-grading usually resolves the majority; re-shooting is reserved for the violations no crop or preset can reach. This audit-first order avoids the common waste of re-shooting material that a preset would have fixed.

## 7. Examples

- **University of Missouri brand photography** directs photographers to "incorporate or simulate warm, natural light and sun-drenched settings" and to "be creative with your angles. Shoot high to evoke grandeur and low for..." — a complete personality (warm, optimistic, energetic) expressed through light and angle rules alone. [search-verified, URL in References]
- **University of Illinois brand photography** instructs: "Use plenty of natural light, but be cautious when photographing toward windows, as the brightness will make it difficult to capture the subject well" — a lighting rule plus its practical failure mode. [search-verified, URL in References]
- **The "street level, high saturation, strong contrast" style** seen in a photography style summary during research ("Our photography style is street level and eye level to the viewer... strong contrast between light and dark") shows how distance, angle, saturation and contrast combine into one voice. [search-verified]
- **Archetypes as starting points** (context-dependent, not exhaustive): documentary warmth (candid people, available light, natural grade — service and community brands); studio cleanliness (controlled light, seamless backgrounds, neutral grade — product and technology brands); atmospheric texture (light, weather and material close-ups, moody grade — hospitality and cultural brands); architectural calm (composed spaces, wide frames, cool neutral grade — finance and law).
- **The drift audit.** An organisation's site mixes commissioned portraits, old event coverage and stock heroes; a six-axis audit finds the violations cluster in white balance (three temperature families) and distance (five habitual camera distances). One shared warm-neutral preset plus a distance rule for future acquisitions fixes 80% of the visible inconsistency without a single reshoot — the audit-first order from section 6 at work.
- **Worked mini case — an autumn festival kit.** From a terracotta-and-sand logo and a "warm, local, hand-made" positioning, the six axes fill in quickly: subjects are people mid-activity (makers, cooks, audiences), never posed line-ups; distance is medium with close-up texture accents; light is golden-hour natural, no flash; the grade is warm-neutral with protected skin tones and a 5% black fade; treatment is reserved for background duotones; ratios are 16:9, 4:5 and 1:1. The one-page spec derived from this becomes the photographer's brief, the stock-buyer's scorecard and the prompt anchor for [AI image generation](ai-image-generation.md) simultaneously — one document, three uses.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Style by adjective only.** "Modern and authentic" with no checkable rules produces drift within a single shoot. Fix by converting every adjective into a decision on one of the six axes.
- **Grade drift between sources.** Commissioned, stock and AI images graded by feel instead of by one preset. Fix with a shared LUT and a contact-sheet review.
- **Mixed colour temperatures.** Warm interior shots next to cool daylight shots in one sequence reads as an error even to non-experts. Fix with a temperature family rule and grading.
- **The generic hero.** A staged stock photo of smiling people pointing at a screen signals "nothing specific to say". Fix via the anti-cliché strategies in [Image selection](image-selection.md).
- **One-off heroics.** A single spectacular image bought outside the system reads as impressive for a week and off-style forever. Fix by rejecting beautiful-but-foreign material, per the coherence principle in section 3.
- **Cropping away the story.** Reusing a wide environmental image cropped to a tight square that slices faces or context. Fix with master files plus documented safe crop zones.
- **Negative space as an afterthought.** Heroes bought with no empty region, then rescued with a heavy overlay. Fix by making text space an acquisition requirement.
- **Over-treating every image.** Duotone, masks and heavy vignettes applied everywhere until the photography becomes wallpaper. Fix with a treatment budget (see [Image treatment](image-treatment.md)).
- **Rights discovered late.** A perfect image with recognisable people, no model release and a launch deadline. Fix by checking clearance status at selection time (see [Image rights](image-rights.md)).
- **Style not carried to external contributors.** A guest photographer, a partner organisation or an AI prompt produced without the one-pager delivers off-style work. Fix by attaching the direction and grading everything on arrival.
- **Reference images drifting from reality.** The approved references depict a lighting family the current venue, season or budget cannot produce; the style keeps being "almost right". Fix by re-anchoring when circumstances change materially rather than forcing an obsolete look.
- **Treating the style as photography-only.** When imagery is sourced or generated without the style rules, the photographed core stays coherent while the acquired margins drift. Fix by applying the six axes to every acquisition path, not only to commissioned shoots.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- The style is written as checkable rules on all six axes (subject, composition, light, grade, treatment, ratios), not only adjectives.
- Three to five reference images exist, ideally as "this, not that" pairs.
- The style one-pager carries a version date, and the library has been audited against the current version.
- One grading preset is defined and applied to every image in the library.
- Colour temperature falls inside the stated family across the current library.
- Camera distance and angle match the stated defaults in a side-by-side review.
- Every master file is archived uncropped at full resolution, with the grade applied only downstream.
- Hero images have planned negative space where the layout needs it.
- Aspect ratios in use come from the declared family table.
- People-centred images are cleared (releases, licence class) before design, not after.
- The direction one-pager travels with every commission, sourcing batch and generation brief.
- Each layout template's image needs are budgeted, and the library has no unassigned "someday" material.
- A contact-sheet review of the last ten images produces no visible mismatches.
