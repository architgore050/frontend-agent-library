# Image selection

## 1. What is it?

Image selection is the craft of deciding which visual — if any — belongs in a given slot, and where that visual comes from. It covers the sourcing question (commission a photographer, buy stock, commission an illustration, generate with AI, or use no image) and the quality question (does this specific image communicate the right thing without misleading, distracting or cheapening the design). Sourcing options and their legal consequences are detailed in [Image rights](image-rights.md); the craft behind photographic content is in [Photography](photography.md); synthesis options are in [AI image generation](ai-image-generation.md).

Selection is a design decision, not a shopping task. The same brief can be filled by four different visual types, and choosing badly — a photograph where an illustration was needed, or an image where empty space was needed — damages the design even if the chosen item is individually excellent.

## 2. Why does it matter?

Images are read before text and carry emotional weight out of proportion to their area. Three failure classes make selection worth treating as a discipline of its own:

- **Misinformation.** An image can lie. A stock photo of strangers presented as "our satisfied customers" is a small deception users and regulators increasingly notice. A photorealistic AI image of an event that never happened can misrepresent reality. Where an image makes a factual claim — this is the venue, this is the product, this is a real attendee — the claim must be true.
- **Cliché.** The handshake, the lightbulb, the whiteboard full of sticky notes, the model holding a credit card: these signal "generic stock" instantly and erode credibility. They persist because they are the easiest results to find.
- **Incoherence.** Ten individually acceptable images from ten sources, treatments and palettes read as a collage rather than a brand. Selection is where coherence is bought or lost, because this is the moment sources are mixed.

There is also a cost dimension: custom photography is expensive but unique and exact; stock is cheap but over-used; generation is fast but rights-ambiguous and drift-prone. Selection is the point where these trade-offs are resolved (see section 5.2).

In the kit-extension scenario this library assumes — a fixed event logo being expanded into a complete brand kit — selection is the step that decides what the world around the mark looks like. The logo constrains palette and personality; selection decides whether the imagery around it documents a real place, evokes an atmosphere, illustrates an idea or leaves space alone. Because the kit will be reused across future events and organisations, the selection method matters more than any single pick: a repeatable five-option ladder and a scorecard keep the next kit coherent without rediscovering why this one worked.

## 3. Core principles

**Communicate, do not decorate.** Every image should either convey information (what the product looks like, what the venue feels like), evoke the intended mood, or both. If an image's only contribution is filling a hole, omitting it is usually the stronger design. John Berger's *Ways of Seeing* frames images as arguments about the world, not neutral decoration — that framing is the selection test. [canonical]

**Specificity beats genericism.** A photograph of the actual venue, the actual product, the actual city beats a beautiful generic equivalent. Search strategy should aim for specifics: "team meeting" loses to "team meeting standing desk workshop brick wall".

**Fit the family, not just the slot.** A candidate image must match the established style on light, colour, distance and energy (see [Photography](photography.md)), or be gradeable into it cheaply.

**Resolve rights before aesthetics.** An unusable image is not an option, so clearance status is part of the selection criteria, not an afterthought. The full licence map is in [Image rights](image-rights.md).

**Selection is a system, not a hunt.** The output of good selection is not one good image but a repeatable method — concrete translation, scorecard, batch review, log — that the next project or the next editor can run without its author. Methods survive across projects and organisations; private intuitions do not.

**Truthfulness has tiers.** Abstract, atmospheric and decorative imagery (textures, gradients, stylised scenes) is freely generative. Imagery that claims to document reality — real people, real products, real events — requires genuine capture or explicit disclosure. Professional convention in editorial and advertising practice treats this distinction as fundamental.

## 4. How it works

### 4.1 Search strategy for stock

Effective stock searching is a funnel, not a single query:

1. **Start conceptual, then narrow to concrete.** Abstract briefs ("growth", "community") return the worst clichés. Translate the concept into concrete scenes first: "growth" becomes "seedling to sapling hands", "community" becomes "people long table meal outdoors". Keep a running list of the concrete translations.
2. **Use negative filtering.** Exclude the known clichés of the topic from results mentally (or by search refinement): posed handshakes, crossing-arm headshots, the same woman laughing at a salad. If the top page of results is all clichés, the query is too generic — go more specific, not less.
3. **Search by craft words.** Adding directional words lifts quality fast: "backlit", "overhead", "candid", "silhouette", "negative space", "shallow depth of field", "aerial". These words correspond to the axes in [Photography](photography.md) and return images by photographers who control them deliberately.
4. **Follow photographers, not queries.** When one image fits, inspect that photographer's portfolio and adjacent contributors. Building a shortlist of three to five compatible photographers is the single most effective consistency technique.
5. **Harvest collections.** Most major platforms offer curated collections and "similar images" paths; a good curated set often beats search for aesthetic-led briefs. On free platforms, the licence terms differ from paid stock — for example Unsplash states images are free for commercial use and that "attribution isn't required", though photographers appreciate it [search-verified, URL in References] — so platform terms must be confirmed per source (see [Image rights](image-rights.md)).
6. **Shortlist in a grid.** Collect 10–20 candidates, then compare them side by side against the style scorecard (section 4.3). Single-image review hides inconsistencies.

### 4.2 Avoiding clichés

- **Search for the specific, not the symbol.** "Handshake" loses to "two builders mid-conversation on scaffolding". The concrete version is harder to find and impossible to mistake for filler.
- **Look past page one.** Clichés cluster at the top of results because they are the most downloaded. Deep pages, reverse-chronological and curated collections hide the distinctive material.
- **Buy the moment, not the pose.** Prefer images of actions, gestures and in-between expressions over posed perfection. Posed is what makes an image read as stock, independent of its licence.
- **Use treatment honestly.** Cropping, grading and duotone can rescue a usable but generic image (see [Image treatment](image-treatment.md)) — but treatment cannot make a meaningless image meaningful. Treat it as the last rescue step, not the first plan.
- **Consider illustration for concepts.** Concepts with no honest photographic form (security, scalability, latency) usually deserve illustration, where metaphor is explicit rather than faked. See [Illustration](../graphic-design/illustration.md).

### 4.3 Consistency techniques when sourcing from stock

- **One photographer where possible** — their lighting and grading instincts come pre-aligned.
- **One palette filter.** Many platforms allow colour filtering; filtering all selections to the brand's dominant colour (or its neutral family) pre-harmonises the set.
- **One crop and treatment system.** Decide the crop family and the grading preset in advance and run every purchased image through it.
- **Batch selection.** Buy the quarter's imagery in one session against the scorecard, rather than ad hoc purchases that drift as the brief erodes.
- **Scorecard.** Five binary checks per candidate: lighting family matches; colour family matches or is gradeable; subject passes the anti-cliché test; composition leaves the layout the negative space it needs; rights class is confirmed usable. Any fail, reject.

A worked pass of the scorecard on two candidates for a workshop-section header:

| Check | Candidate A (sunlit room, candid) | Candidate B (studio group, posed) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lighting family | Pass — natural window light | Fail — flat studio flash |
| Colour family | Pass — warm, gradeable | Pass — neutral, gradeable |
| Anti-cliché | Pass — mid-action, unposed | Fail — the classic posed team shot |
| Negative space | Pass — clear left third | Fail — background fills the frame |
| Rights class | Pass — commercial licence confirmed | Pass — commercial licence confirmed |
| Verdict | **Select** | Reject |

The table is deliberately binary: candidate B loses on three checks despite being professionally shot, because consistency is decided by the rules, not by polish. A rejection is also logged with its reason — the accumulated reasons become the team's private anti-cliché dictionary.

### 4.4 Custom versus stock

| Criterion | Custom (commissioned) | Stock / free platforms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniqueness | Total; competitors cannot use it | Shared; the same image appears elsewhere |
| Exactness | Matches brief, venue, product, people | Approximates the brief at best |
| Cost | High (crew, location, usage licences) | Low per image; subscription models |
| Time | Days to weeks | Minutes |
| Consistency control | Full, if art-directed well | Partial; depends on selection discipline |
| Rights | Clean if contracted; releases handled at shoot | Must verify licence class per platform |
| Risk | Budget and schedule overruns | Cliché, incoherence, licence confusion |

As a context-dependent rule of thumb: commission for hero imagery and anything that must show your actual product, people or place; source from stock for supporting and volume imagery (articles, backgrounds, social fillers); never let stock stand in for something the audience can fact-check.

### 4.5 Volume planning

Selection is easier when the quantity is decided before the sourcing starts. Walk the page and template inventory, and fix an image budget per template type: how many photographs the home page truly needs, whether article bodies carry one image or none, how many distinct backgrounds the social templates require. Three consequences follow. First, the budget exposes where images are decoration — a template that "needs six images" usually needs one strong one and better typography. Second, it sizes the sourcing effort honestly: a quarter's supporting imagery is one batch session against the scorecard, not twenty ad hoc searches. Third, it concentrates spend where images do real work, which is what makes the custom-versus-stock trade-off in section 4.4 decidable rather than emotional. Budgets belong with the layout side ([Grid systems](../layout/grid-systems.md)) and should be revisited when templates change.

## 5. Practical application

### 5.1 The five-option decision framework

For every image slot, work through these questions in order. The first "yes" on the generation ladder usually settles the option; the truthfulness ladder can veto it.

**Step 1 — Should this slot carry an image at all?** If the slot exists only to fill space, or the surrounding content already communicates, omit. Omission is a legitimate and often stronger option: white space improves legibility and lets stronger images breathe (see [Spacing and proportion](../layout/spacing-and-proportion.md)). Omit also whenever a truthful image cannot be obtained and no honest abstraction exists.

**Step 2 — Does the image claim to document reality?** If the image will be read as "this actually happened / this is actually them / this is actually the venue" (news-adjacent announcements, testimonials, team pages, event coverage), it must come from real capture. A generated or staged stand-in presented as documentation is misinformation. This is an established professional and editorial convention, increasingly enforced by platform policy and law.

**Step 3 — Is the subject physically available and budgetable?** If the thing to be shown exists and can be photographed within budget and schedule, commission photography (exact, unique, rights-clean). If it exists but capture is impractical (hazards, scale, cost, access), consider stock of the real class of thing, or illustration if specificity does not matter.

**Step 4 — Is the content imaginative, abstract or impossible to photograph?** Futures, metaphors, interior states, styles that need fusion ("a lighthouse made of paper") are the territory of illustration or AI generation. Choose illustration when stylistic control, scalability and clean rights matter most and the brand already has an illustration voice; choose generation for speed, photographic realism and cheap iteration — accepting the consistency and rights overheads of [AI image generation](ai-image-generation.md).

**Step 5 — Could a non-photographic element do the job better?** Texture, gradient, pattern or typography alone frequently outperform a forced image, especially in tight systems. See [Patterns and textures](../graphic-design/patterns-and-textures.md).

The five options summarised:

| Option | Choose when | Key risk | Relative cost / time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Photograph (commission) | Reality must be documented; hero imagery; exact subjects needed | Cost, schedule | High cost / slow |
| Source (stock or free platform) | Supporting imagery; speed matters; subject class is generic | Cliché, licence confusion, incoherence | Low cost / fast |
| Generate (AI) | Photorealistic or experimental imagery; iteration speed valued; no factual claim made | Rights ambiguity, style drift, artefacts | Low direct cost / fast, plus QA time |
| Illustrate (commission or library) | Abstract concepts; strong system voice; long-term scalable asset | Style lock-in, cost of custom work | Medium cost / medium |
| Omit | No truthful or fitting image; space serves better empty | — | Zero |

Worked example — extending a fixed event logo into a full kit (the library's reference use case): the hero needs the real venue (photograph it); the speakers page needs the real speakers (commission portraits under the [photography style](photography.md)); the schedule background needs atmosphere with no factual claim (generate or texture); the "about the festival" page needs energy and community (source, batch, grade); a quote card needs no image at all (type and colour suffice).

### 5.2 Cost / time / quality axes

When the decision is close, score the top two options on three axes: **cost** (direct spend plus rights and clearance effort), **time** (elapsed calendar time to a final cleared asset) and **quality** (fit to brief, uniqueness, and coherence with the existing library). Selection fails most often when one axis is optimised alone — the cheapest coherent set beats the most beautiful incoherent one; the fastest unique asset beats the slowest perfect one only if the deadline is real.

## 6. Decision framework

Condensed selection flow:

1. Slot has no communicative job → **omit**.
2. Image claims factual reality → real capture or don't publish it.
3. Subject is real, available, budgeted → **commission photography**.
4. Subject class is generic and supporting → **source**, batch, grade.
5. Subject is abstract, imaginary or fused → **illustrate** (control, rights, system fit) or **generate** (speed, realism, iteration).
6. Everything else considered, and a non-photographic element is stronger → omit or use texture/colour.

Evaluate the chosen option against: truthfulness (no false implication), fit (passes the five-point scorecard), rights (confirmed licence class and releases — [Image rights](image-rights.md)), and coherence (indistinguishable in tone from the rest of the library in a contact-sheet review).

A review habit worth institutionalising: keep the ladder and scorecard on one page next to the [photography style](photography.md) one-pager, so selection, treatment and generation all answer to the same document. When a decision is genuinely ambiguous, the tie-breaker is communication value — the option that makes the message land with the fewest assumptions wins, and if none does, omission wins.

## 7. Examples

- **The anti-pattern hero.** Brief "innovative conference" returns a globe-on-a-circuit-board stock image. Fix: translate the concept to concrete ("audience leaning forward during a talk, backlit stage") and search that; or omit the hero and lead with typography.
- **The fake testimonial.** A layout needs "customer quote cards" with faces; sourcing strangers' portraits and labelling them as customers is misinformation even with a valid licence. Fix: real customer photos with consent (and releases — [Image rights](image-rights.md)), or design the cards without portraits.
- **The impossible visual.** "Data flowing through the platform" has no photographable form. Fix: commission illustration for a durable, ownable visual (see [Illustration](../graphic-design/illustration.md)), or generate throwaway concept art first to test directions before paying for custom work.
- **The cohesive free-platform set.** A small event brand with no image budget builds its library from one free platform, filtered to one photographer's work and one colour family, then runs everything through the same grading preset — cheap, fast and coherent, with licence terms confirmed before use (Unsplash's licence, for instance, allows free commercial use without required attribution) [search-verified, URL in References].
- **The concept that earned its keep.** "Accessibility" has no honest photograph; stock returns the wheelchair-symbol clichés. The team commissions one illustration — a stylised ramp as a graphic device reusable across the system — which becomes a durable asset instead of a disposable page decoration ([Illustration](../graphic-design/illustration.md)).
- **The omission that improved the page.** A dense schedule page was designed with a decorative image slot nobody could fill well; removing it left a calm typographic grid that read faster. The slot-level decision ladder's first question — "should this carry an image at all?" — did the work.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Decorative filler.** Images added to "break up text" that carry no meaning and fight the message. Fix by the omission test in section 5.1.
- **The style by accumulation.** Letting the set of purchased images define the style retroactively, instead of sourcing against a defined style. Fix by writing the direction first ([Photography](photography.md)).
- **Fact-checkable stand-ins.** Stock or generated images standing in for the actual venue, product or people. Audiences and press notice; fix at selection time.
- **Cliché blindness.** Accepting the first page of results. Fix with concrete translation and deep/curated searching.
- **Source mixing without a unifier.** Five platforms, five grades. Fix with the preset and scorecard.
- **Licensing by assumption.** "It appeared free in search" or "the platform said free to use, so this use is fine". Fix by reading the licence class per source ([Image rights](image-rights.md)).
- **Buying singles.** Ad hoc purchases that drift from the style. Fix with batch sessions.
- **Budget-less sourcing.** No fixed quantity per template, so imagery accumulates until the library is unmaintainable and the style is diluted by volume. Fix with the volume plan in section 4.5.
- **Ignoring the miss.** Proceeding with a mediocre image because the slot "needs something". Fix by re-running the decision ladder — omitting is allowed.
- **Selection amnesia.** Each project re-searches from scratch because the previous method — search terms that worked, photographers shortlisted, rejections logged — was never written down. Fix by keeping the method artefact next to the rights log so the next selection starts ahead.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- Every image slot passed the five-option ladder, and omission was genuinely considered.
- No image makes a factual claim that capture or disclosure does not support.
- The stock set shares a photographer, palette, crop family or treatment system — at least one unifier beyond the platform.
- Anti-cliché checks were applied (concrete search terms, past page one, posed imagery avoided).
- Custom-versus-stock decisions are documented with the cost/time/quality reasoning.
- Rights status of each selection is recorded at selection time (source, licence, date — see [Image rights](image-rights.md)).
- The assembled set survives a side-by-side grid review without visible style breaks.
- Each selected image passes the five-point scorecard (light, colour, cliché, negative space, rights).
- Rejections are logged with reasons, building the team's working anti-cliché list.
- An image budget per template exists, and the sourcing batch was sized against it.
- Where a sourced image is used, the platform's own licence — not an assumption — was the basis for the use.
- The selection method (funnel, scorecard, batch review) is written down where the next editor can find it.
- Selection, treatment, generation and rights all answer to the same one-page style document.
- Where a slot could not be filled well, omission was chosen deliberately and recorded.
