# Academic Design Curricula

## 1. What is this reference?

This file surveys real design-education programmes — what they actually teach, in what sequence, through which
exercises, and towards which stated competencies. It is a documentary record of publicly accessible curricula plus a
synthesis: a reconstructed curriculum for an agent-designer, mapped onto the directories of this repository. It exists
so that an agent extending a logo into a full brand kit, or planning any design task, can borrow the structure that
design schools use to build competence — rather than improvising a learning path from scattered tips.

Two cautions frame everything below. First, a course page or programme description is a compressed statement of intent,
not the full teaching material; lecture notes, briefs and critiques are usually behind enrolment. Second, what follows
reports only what the accessed pages actually state — where a source was not reachable, that is recorded explicitly
rather than reconstructed from memory or reputation.

## 2. Why does it matter for an agent-designer?

Design schools encode, refined over decades, the order in which skills should be acquired and the exercises that
reliably produce them.

- RISD sequences its BFA from visual principles (form, image, colour, typography) to communication theory and visual
  systems, and only then to applied practice.
- The Ulm School built an entire methodology on the idea that design is a discipline integrating art with science, not a
  talent show.
- John Cabot's branding course turns identity design into a repeatable pipeline from client questionnaire to final
  presentation.

An agent that internalises these sequences can structure its own work the same way: establish foundations before
identity, analyse before ideating, and present and critique at defined milestones.

The reconstructed curriculum also serves as a coverage check. When the repository's directories are treated as a
syllabus, gaps in an agent's plan for a brand-extension project become visible early — for example, discovering that a
plan covers logo and colour but never addresses print production or governance, two topics that the surveyed curricula
treat as non-optional.

## 4. Programme profiles

### 4.1 John Cabot University — Graphic Design: Corporate Identity and Branding (AS 330 H)

**Accessed:** the printed syllabus at
`https://myjcu.johncabot.edu/syllabus/syllabus_print.aspx?IDS=16395`
(Spring 2022 offering, instructor Sabrina Schmidt). This is the most
operationally detailed source in the survey and the direct model for the
identity-design pipeline in Section 6.

#### Basic facts as stated

- Honors course, 4 semester hours of credit (the credit field lists 3), Wednesday studio sessions totalling 45 contact
  hours.
- Prerequisite: one previous graphic design course (including AS 232) plus a minimum cumulative GPA of 3.5 for the
  honours designation.
- Studio-based — the syllabus states "this class cannot be taken remotely" — and assumes existing proficiency in major
  graphic-design software.
- Course scope: responding to a design brief's technical and communication requirements; developing visual concepts;
  building a system of graphic elements that forms an identity; defining brand strategy; professional print-production
  standards.

#### Stated learning outcomes

Students learn to:

1. edit a client questionnaire;
2. work with a creative brief;
3. conduct and present a competitor analysis;
4. interpret a brand's tone and build a mood board;
5. create a colour story;
6. choose typography;
7. design supporting graphics;
8. build a professional client presentation.

#### Course calendar as stated

The syllabus publishes a full 15-week schedule. Its shape is as instructive as its content: four numbered exercises in
multi-week blocks, with short "insight" lectures interleaved, one formal guest critique, and print production at the
end.

| Week(s) | Stated content |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Course overview, intro project, branding essentials, client-questionnaire editing |
| 2 | The creative brief; insight on visual identity |
| 3–4 | Exercise #1; resources and techniques |
| 5–8 | Exercise #2, continued across four sessions; insight on the characteristics of a logo, then insight on typography |
| 9–10 | Exercise #3; resources and techniques; creative growth; preparing for critique |
| 11 | Guest crit |
| 12 | Exercise #3 continued |
| 13–14 | Exercise #4; insight on print |
| 15 | Final exam (a design project) |

#### Assessment as stated

The standard track is five components worth 20% each:

1. mood board and colour story;
2. logo and typographic system;
3. graphic language;
4. brand identity collage;
5. final exam — a design project for the new brand identity.

The honours track replaces these with two brand-related design projects at 50% each. Grading criteria tie the top grade
to mastery, exceptional technical skill, originality and collaboration, and penalise attendance problems. Note the
ordering discipline: the mood board is worth as much as the logo, and the capstone is the presentation of a system, not
the mark.

#### Recommended bibliography as listed

- Per Mollerup, *Marks of Excellence: The History and Taxonomy of Trademarks* (Phaidon, 1999).
- David Gibbs, *Pentagram: The Compendium* (Phaidon, 1998).
- Emil Ruder, *Typography* (Hastings House, 2009 edition).
- Bruno Munari, *Design as Art* (Penguin Classics, 2008 edition).

These four titles are a useful canonical reading spine for identity work — trademark taxonomy, practitioner compendium,
typographic craft, and design attitude respectively.

### 4.2 UCLL (University of Leuven/Limburg) — Brand Identity and Graphic Design

**Accessed:** the programme page for *Marketing – Track in Belgium (IBM)* at
`https://www.ucll.be/nl/professionele-bachelor/marketing-track-belgium-ibm-engelstalig-traject`.

#### Programme facts as stated

- A three-year, 180-ECTS professional bachelor taught in English at EQF level 6, with "high practical orientation"
  through cases, company projects and a compulsory (international) internship.
- Graduates are prepared for roles including marketing/product/brand/ communication manager and social-media specialist.

#### Where branding sits in the curriculum

*Brand Identity and Graphic Design* is a 4-credit course in Phase 2 (second year), grouped under the *Marketing
Communication* cluster alongside Digital Marketing courses. Copywriting appears in the same year; Visual Storytelling (3
credits) and Webdesign (4 credits) are already taken in Phase 1; Integrated Marketing Communication follows in Phase 3,
where Design Thinking also appears.

This placement is itself informative: the school teaches identity design not as fine art but as a
communication-management skill, embedded in a pipeline that starts with visual storytelling and ends with integrated
communication practice. An agent working on a brand kit for a marketing context can borrow this framing — the identity
is a component of communication strategy, not a standalone artwork.

#### Access limitation

The programme page gives the course title and credit weight but no syllabus content — no topics, objectives or
exercises. A fuller course overview exists on UCLL's onderwijsaanbod subdomain but was not fetched. A LinkedIn profile
appearing in search results attributes the teaching of the course to Thomas Van Ryckeghem in the second and third
bachelor years [search-verified]; no course content was taken from it.

### 4.3 Rhode Island School of Design — BFA Graphic Design

**Accessed:** the programme page at
`https://www.risd.edu/academics/graphic-design/bachelors-program`.

#### Curriculum sequence as stated

A four-year BFA grounded in "design principles, theories of communication and strategies for problem solving",
progressing in three stages:

1. **Sophomore year** — visual principles of "form, image, color and typography" (source's spelling).
2. **Junior year** — conceptual thinking focused on "communication theory, visual systems and information design".
3. **Senior year** — design applications, with electives exposing students to specific areas of graphic-design practice.

Juniors and seniors may pursue professional internships, and the programme culminates in a self-defined degree project.

#### Stated graduate competencies

Graduates are prepared to:

- shape typography, image and content in response to context, audience and communication objectives;
- apply methods for form-making — visual research, ideation, experimentation, iteration, curation, interpretation;
- work with contemporary and traditional tools across digital and analog technologies;
- create, analyse and critique visual communication from historical, social and intersectional viewpoints;
- develop personal methodologies and a cohesive body of work;
- apply visual systems across print, digital and 3D media;
- enter professional practice with presentation, communication and collaboration skills.

#### Studio context as stated

One of RISD's largest departments (roughly 150 undergraduate majors), with facilities for digital and traditional
printing, bookbinding, papermaking and screenprinting. Project types range from books, magazines and posters to
UX/websites, film graphics, identities, packaging and exhibits.

#### Catalogue detail [search-verified]

The RISD course-catalogue page
(`https://risd.coursedog.com/programs/f18f2c7d2b301000dff362d69eaf0001`)
shows the typography requirement as a three-course, 9-credit sequence
(Type I, Type II, Type III) plus 12 credits of introductory design studios.
This confirms what the programme page summarises: typography is taught as a
sustained multi-year thread, not a single module.

### 4.4 Ulm School of Design (HfG Ulm), 1953–1968 — the historical model

**Accessed:** the encyclopaedia article at
`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulm_School_of_Design` (a tertiary source;
details below are its statements, useful for the pedagogical model rather
than archival precision).

#### Founding and ethos

Founded in 1953 by Inge Aicher-Scholl, Otl Aicher and Max Bill (first rector, a former Bauhaus student); teaching began
in August 1953 in rooms of the Ulm Volkshochschule, and the Max Bill-designed building was inaugurated in October 1955.

Under Tomás Maldonado, design was framed as "a system embodying both scientific-based and intuitive-based thinking". The
school integrated sociology, psychology, politics, economics, philosophy and systems thinking with aesthetics and
technology, rather than continuing the Bauhaus artist form-giving model. Critics inside the school argued design "must
be a balancing of both art and science", which is the famous legacy position (the oft-quoted "50:50" formulation does
not appear verbatim on this page).

#### Departments and structure

Five departments: Product Design; Visual Communication (renamed from "Visual Design" in 1956/57); Industrialized
Building; Information (until 1962); and Filmmaking (part of Visual Communication until 1961).

The four-year pedagogy:

1. a mandatory first-year basic course (Vorkurs) covering visual experiments (symmetry, topology), workshop practice
   (wood, metal, plastics, photography), presentation skills (drawing, writing, language) and methodology (logic,
   mathematics, combinatorics, topology);
2. specialisation in a department in years two and three;
3. a thesis year last.

#### Methodological legacy

The school pioneered semiotics in design teaching; product design added ergonomics, human–machine systems, statistics
and mechanics. Its industrial "development groups" produced audio equipment with Braun (with Dieter Rams, establishing
the "Braun style"), corporate identity for Lufthansa, and elevated-train design for the Hamburg railroad — the strongest
historical precedent for treating corporate identity as a system problem.

Funding was withdrawn in November 1968 and the school closed, but the "Ulm Model" shaped design education
internationally.

#### Why an agent should care

Ulm's contribution to this curriculum is the conviction that design decisions must be justifiable — through logic,
methodology and science — and that identity work (Lufthansa) is system-building governed by rules, not one-off artwork.
That is precisely the stance an agent-designer needs.

## 5. Patterns across the programmes

This section is original synthesis of the profiles above. Four recurring structures stand out, and each carries a lesson
for agent-designed workflows:

### 5.1 Foundations before application

RISD spends a full year on form, image, colour and typography before communication theory; Ulm spends a full year on the
Vorkurs before specialisation. Neither treats craft fundamentals as optional warm-up. The implication: an agent should
establish typographic, colour and compositional parameters before generating identity artefacts, not in parallel with
them.

### 5.2 Analysis before making

John Cabot's course opens with the client questionnaire and creative brief, then competitor analysis, then mood board
and colour story — the first graded deliverable is the mood board, not the logo. Identity work is a pipeline in which
the logo arrives only after strategy artefacts exist. This mirrors the discovery-then-design structure in [Brand
discovery](../branding/brand-discovery.md).

### 5.3 Typographic depth as a spine

RISD's three-semester typography sequence and John Cabot's interleaved "insight on typography" sessions both treat type
as a continuously developed skill. For an agent this argues for consulting [Typography
fundamentals](../typography/typography-fundamentals.md) and its siblings at every identity decision, not once.

### 5.4 Public critique as a built-in stage

John Cabot schedules a guest critique as a fixed calendar event; RISD lists critique among its stated competencies.
Design education assumes that work is evaluated against explicit criteria before completion — the basis for the
evaluation checklists in this repository (see [Design quality framework](../evaluation/design-quality-framework.md)).

### 5.5 Comparison at a glance

| Programme | Type | Structure evidence | Signature feature | Source strength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| John Cabot AS 330 H | Single university course | Full 15-week calendar + graded deliverables | Assignment pipeline from questionnaire to presentation | Primary syllabus, fetched |
| UCLL Brand Identity & GD | Course inside a business degree | Catalogue position and credits only | Branding taught as communication management, EQF 6 practice-oriented | Programme page, fetched; no syllabus |
| RISD BFA | Four-year art-school degree | Year-by-year sequence + competencies | Three-semester typography spine; degree project | Programme page, fetched; catalogue search-verified |
| HfG Ulm | Historical school model | Four-year Vorkurs→specialisation→thesis | Art–science methodology; industrial development groups (Braun, Lufthansa) | Encyclopaedia article, fetched |
| HfG Gmünd | Contemporary successor | Roots/lineage only | Continuity of the Ulm tradition | Search-verified snippet only |

### 6.2 What competence looks like (assessment signals)

A curriculum is only useful if the agent can test itself against it. For each competency, the signal below describes
what a graded piece of work would demonstrate. These signals are original synthesis, modelled on the grading logic
visible in the JCU syllabus (mastery, technical skill, originality, quantity of work) and RISD's stated outcomes.

1. **Perception** — the agent can name the compositional devices at work in an existing artefact (weight, axis,
   grouping, contrast) without seeing a written rationale for it.
2. **Typography** — hierarchy is explicit and checkable: scale steps, weights and spacing values exist for every text
   level, and typeface choices have stated reasons tied to the brief.
3. **Colour** — the palette has roles (primary, accent, functional states), contrast relationships are verified against
   accessibility requirements, and the colour story is argued from the brand's tone.
4. **Layout** — spacing and alignment derive from a stated grid or scale, and the system survives being applied to an
   unseen content set.
5. **Pipeline** — strategy artefacts (questionnaire, brief, competitor analysis) exist *before* mark and palette
   decisions and visibly shaped them.
6. **Mark extension** — the fixed logo's construction, colour and character have been analysed, and derived marks are
   compatible rather than new designs ([Fixed logo analysis](../branding/fixed-logo-analysis.md)).
7. **Graphic language** — supporting graphics share one construction logic (weight, corner style, density) with the logo
   rather than being stylistic strangers.
8. **Information design** — dense material is ordered by a documented hierarchy, not by layout accident.
9. **Applications** — at least one artefact per named surface exists, each adapted to its medium rather than resized.
10. **Codification** — a third party could reproduce the identity from the documentation without asking questions.
11. **Critique** — the agent can evaluate its own output against explicit criteria and name what it changed after each
    round.
12. **Inclusion** — accessibility checks are part of the deliverable, not an optional extra.

### 6.3 The exercise ladder (adapted from John Cabot's assignment structure)

The JCU syllabus is valuable because its five graded deliverables form a complete identity pipeline. Adapted for an
agent, each rung produces a checkable artefact:

1. **Client questionnaire and creative brief** — write the questions, answer them from available material, summarise the
   brief in writing before any visual work. Rung one exists precisely to prevent jumping to visuals (see [Brand
   discovery](../branding/brand-discovery.md)).
2. **Competitor analysis** — a presented comparison of adjacent identities' colour, type and graphic conventions; feeds
   differentiation decisions.
3. **Mood board and colour story** — tone established and translated into a palette rationale before any logo work
   ([Moodboards](../methodology/moodboards.md), [Palette development](../colour/palette-development.md)).
4. **Logo and typographic system** — mark plus primary/secondary typefaces with hierarchy rules ([Logo
   systems](../branding/logo-systems.md), [Typographic hierarchy](../typography/typographic-hierarchy.md)).
5. **Graphic language** — supporting graphics, icon style, imagery treatment, applied across named applications
   ([Graphic language](../graphic-design/graphic-language.md)).
6. **Brand identity collage and final presentation** — everything assembled and presented as a system, the format JCU
   grades as its final exam ([Brand guidelines](../branding/brand-guidelines.md),
   [Presentations](../applications/presentations.md)).

Note the ordering discipline: in JCU's grading, the mood board is worth as much as the logo (20% each), and the capstone
is presentation, not logo. An agent that spends 80% of its effort on the mark and 5 minutes on application and
presentation is out of step with how identity design is actually taught and assessed.

### 6.4 A reconstructed fifteen-week schedule

Mapping JCU's stated calendar (Section 4.1) onto an agent's project plan for a single identity project produces this
rhythm. Week labels follow JCU's actual sequence; the agent-adapted action column is original synthesis.

| Week | JCU pattern | Agent-adapted action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Overview; branding essentials; questionnaire | Assemble brief inputs; write the questionnaire and answer it from available material |
| 2 | Creative brief; visual-identity insight | Freeze the written brief; list constraints (fixed logo, media list, deadlines) |
| 3–4 | Exercise #1 | Mood board and colour story; verify palette contrast |
| 5–8 | Exercise #2 with logo and typography insights | Logo-system analysis and typographic system; hierarchy rules written down |
| 9–10 | Exercise #3; preparing for critique | Graphic language: icons, patterns, imagery treatment; draft applications |
| 11 | Guest crit | Structured self-critique against the brand identity checklist; record verdicts |
| 12 | Exercise #3 continued | Apply critique verdicts to the graphic language |
| 13–14 | Exercise #4; insight on print | Production pass: print margins/bleed checks, export formats, accessibility pass |
| 15 | Final exam | Final presentation of the complete system; guideline document handed over |

The lesson encoded in this rhythm: critique sits in the middle of the schedule, not at the end, and production concerns
(print, formats) are a dedicated phase, not an afterthought.

### 6.5 Sequencing for the repository's key use case

For the flagship scenario — extending an already-fixed event logo into a complete brand kit — the reconstructed
curriculum prescribes this order:

1. analyse the fixed mark first (competency 6, [Fixed logo analysis](../branding/fixed-logo-analysis.md));
2. extract colour and tonal direction (competencies 2–3);
3. define typographic hierarchy and pairing (competency 2);
4. build the graphic language and imagery treatment (competency 7);
5. design applications around the fixed mark rather than through it (competency 9);
6. codify everything into guideline form with governance notes (competency 10);
7. then critique against the brand identity checklist (competency 11).

Ulm's lesson applies throughout: every choice should be justifiable by stated reasoning, not taste alone.

## 7. How to use this file in practice

- **As a planning checklist.** Before any identity project, verify that the competency map's twelve rows are either
  covered by the plan or consciously out of scope.
- **As an ordering rule.** When in doubt about sequence, follow the exercise ladder; it encodes the dependency structure
  (strategy before mark, mark before system, system before applications).
- **As a depth reference.** When the agent needs to know what a competent output contains for a competency, the linked
  repository module is the working document; this file only says when to invoke it.

## 8. Common mistakes and limitations of this survey

- **Treating a course title as a syllabus.** The UCLL case shows the trap:
  "Brand Identity and Graphic Design" is fully documented at catalogue level
  (4 credits, Phase 2) yet its page states no topics or outcomes. Do not
  infer teaching content from a title.
- **Assuming named programmes are accessible.** The brief suggested Royal
  College of Art and ArtCenter as candidates; no verifiable URLs for their
  curricula surfaced in this session's tool output, so they are excluded
  rather than paraphrased from reputation.
- **Reading history as a template.** HfG Ulm closed in 1968 and its Wikipedia
  profile is a tertiary source; its model inspires the methodology emphasis
  here but its department names and year structure should not be copied as
  current best practice.
- **Unaccessed primary material.** JCU's course archive pages
  (`https://www.johncabot.edu/academics/course-schedules-syllabi`,
  `https://www.johncabot.edu/academics/past-course-schedules-syllabi`) and
  the RISD catalogue link above were seen but not opened; UCLL's detailed
  onderwijsaanbod course overview was not fetched. None of their contents
  are cited.
- **Grade-of-source mixing.** Statements here mix primary syllabus text
  (JCU), institutional programme pages (RISD, UCLL), and an encyclopaedia
  article (Ulm). Weight claims accordingly: syllabus facts are strongest;
  the Ulm section is for orientation.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- [ ] Every programme claim in a deliverable can be traced to a URL recorded in Section 11, with the right access tag.
- [ ] No teaching content has been inferred from a course title alone.
- [ ] The competency map (6.1) has been consulted and each row consciously addressed or scoped out.
- [ ] The exercise ladder order (6.3) was respected, or deviation from it is justified in writing.
- [ ] Historical material (Ulm) is used for methodology orientation only, not as a source of current numeric guidance.
- [ ] Canonical books from the JCU bibliography are cited as works, never with invented quotes or page numbers.
