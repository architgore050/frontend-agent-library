# Case Studies

## 1. What is this file?

This file applies the repository's design theory to real identities and design systems. Each case study answers the same ten questions — what the organisation communicates, its apparent brand personality, logo system, colour system, typography, graphic language, layout conventions, how the identity is applied, what makes the system consistent, and what can be learned — so that the cases are comparable and the lessons extractable. The purpose is not to admire the examples but to harvest *transferable procedures*: an agent extending a fixed logo into a full brand kit ([Logo systems](../branding/logo-systems.md), [Visual identity](../branding/visual-identity.md)) works by analogy, and analogies are only as good as the analysed evidence behind them.

Ten cases are covered: two public design systems (GOV.UK, USWDS), one university identity examined in unusual depth (MIT), two technology-company identities (Uber, Spotify), three product design systems built around tokens (Material Design, Atlassian, IBM Carbon), one open-source identity (Ubuntu), and one editorial identity (The Guardian, included to cover the mission's editorial priority under an explicit general-knowledge caveat, since no editorial guideline page could be fetched this session). Event branding — a mission priority — receives a deliberately reduced treatment: no event brand page could be fetched this session, so section 4.11 records only the event-branding sources that were verified to exist as search results and the governance lessons their *existence* supports. For event-specific application see [Event collateral](../applications/event-collateral.md).

## 2. Why case studies matter

Design theory tells an agent what hierarchy, colour harmony and consistency *are*; case studies show what those principles look like when an organisation with real constraints — legal risk, multi-team scaling, print and screen contexts, community contributors — acts on them. Three things are visible only in real systems. First, the *coupling between strategy and tokens*: a brand that must communicate trust under scrutiny (government, universities) ends up with restrained palettes and conservative type, which is a strategic consequence, not a taste preference. Second, the *maintenance mechanism*: every long-lived case here carries a governance layer — contribution criteria, do/don't pages, named maintainers — proving that consistency is produced by process, not by the original design burst (see [Brand governance](../branding/brand-governance.md)). Third, the *failure surface*: do/don't pages document precisely the misuses that actually happen, which is the best available evidence for an agent's own "common mistakes" lists.

Case studies also calibrate scope. The cases here span a single-logo department kit (Buffalo-style university rules), a nine-product platform brand (Uber), a community of thousands of volunteers (Ubuntu) and a system serving nearly 200 federal sites (USWDS). Seeing the same ten questions answered at every scale is what lets an agent size its own answers: a two-person event team should not import GOV.UK's contribution pipeline, and a national service should not import Spotify's campaign energy. Selection followed two rules: every case had to be either directly fetched, already cited in the repository, or so widely documented that its inclusion could be honestly labelled general knowledge — and the set had to cover the mission's priority categories with the gaps (event branding) stated rather than papered over.

## 3. How to read these case studies

Each case follows the ten-question structure, in the same order, so cases can be read side by side. Every case opens with an **evidence base** note stating exactly what was verified this session, what is cited from elsewhere in the repository, and what rests on general professional knowledge — because an agent reusing a "fact" from a case study must know its provenance. Provenance tags follow the definitions in [Source accessibility](source-accessibility.md). Three reading rules apply:

1. **Numbers are evidence; vibes are hypotheses.** Where a case gives a numeric rule (MIT's 1-inch clear space, the 30–40% acronym-to-seal ratio), it can be reused directly. Personality descriptions are *apparent* personalities — defensible inferences from visible artefacts, not quotes from brand books.
2. **Extract mechanisms, not palettes.** Copying Material's purple or GOV.UK's black teaches nothing. The transferable content is *why* the palette is narrow, *how* roles are named, and *what* enforcement makes it stick.
3. **Respect the context.** A government service must serve every citizen, including disabled users, under audit; a music-streaming brand must stand out in a feed. A lesson transfers only when the constraint transfers. Tag conclusions as established principle, professional convention, context-dependent choice or house style, per the epistemic rules in the [Bibliography](bibliography.md).

A fourth discipline is knowing how much each case can carry, which the evidence tiers make explicit:

| Evidence tier | Cases | Safe to reuse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fetched this session | GOV.UK, USWDS, MIT | Specific rules, numbers and governance facts as of 2026-11-24 |
| Repository-cited pages, not fetched | Uber, Spotify, Material, Atlassian, Carbon | Structures and mechanisms; verify specifics against the live pages |
| General professional knowledge only | Ubuntu, The Guardian | High-level patterns; verify every specific before reuse |
| Search-verified leads only | Event branding: IOC brand manual, Milano Cortina 2026 look archive, USOPC co-brand rules (section 4.11) | That the sources exist and their apparent scope; nothing specific inside them |

The ten questions work best as an audit template. Applied to any identity — including one the agent is building itself — they force the questions that unstructured browsing skips: *what never changes?* (logo, colour, type) and *who enforces it?* (governance, distribution, licensing). Section 5.6 collects the per-case answers to these two questions into a comparison table.

A fifth discipline governs reuse across sessions: case facts decay. Governance facts in particular are snapshots — GOV.UK Frontend was v6.6.0 on the access date and will not stay so, USWDS's v3.14.0 will advance, and brand pages move without notice. Before reusing a specific number, name or version from any case in a new deliverable, re-fetch the case's evidence-base URL (the re-verification procedure in [Source accessibility](source-accessibility.md), section 9.1, applies unchanged) and date-stamp the fact in the deliverable ("v6.6.0 as of 2026-11-24") so the next reader can judge its age at a glance. A lesson whose constraint still holds does not need re-verification; a lesson whose *figure* is load-bearing always does.

### 4.1 GOV.UK Design System (UK Government Digital Service) — public design system

**Evidence base:** the system's homepage was fetched and read this session (`design-system.service.gov.uk`, accessed 2026-11-24, and re-fetched for confirmation later the same day). The homepage does not enumerate typeface names, colour values or spacing units — those live on linked pages that were not fetched — so details below marked *general knowledge* must be re-verified on the live site before reuse.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** The system's stated purpose is to make government services consistent with GOV.UK and to let teams "avoid repeating work that's already been done". The communication goal is trust and usability at population scale: every service, from tax to visas, should feel like one institution speaking. Consistency is explicitly positioned as a user benefit, not a bureaucratic preference — the fetch made that framing the first sentence of the site.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Plain-spoken, institutional, unhurried. Nothing decorative survives; every element must justify itself by user need. The personality is a direct consequence of the stated purpose: when the audience is every citizen and the stakes are legal rights and obligations, restraint *is* the brand.
3. **Logo system.** The GOV.UK wordmark and crest are managed centrally by the Government Digital Service rather than redesigned per service; service teams start from the shared page template rather than composing their own masthead (general knowledge, not stated on the fetched page). The structural lesson holds regardless of the mark's specifics: one canonical masthead, consumed as a component.
4. **Colour system.** A deliberately small palette with semantic roles — links, error, success, warning — documented on the Colour page (not fetched this session; *general knowledge*: the system's black-on-white body and single blue link colour are widely published hallmarks). The lesson is role-based naming over value-based naming, mirrored in [Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md): a component asks for "the error colour", not for a hex code. Boundary: role-based naming earns its overhead only when colour is consumed by more than one surface or team; a single-page project can safely hard-code three values.
5. **Typography.** The homepage links to pages on Typeface, Type scale, Headings, Paragraphs and font-override classes, but names no face. *General knowledge*: the system uses the GDS Transport typeface, published only for use on government domains, which is itself a governance decision worth noting — licensing as enforcement. When the typeface is contractually confined to its context, inconsistency becomes legally impossible rather than merely discouraged. Boundary: this mechanism is available only to organisations whose scale justifies a bespoke licence; smaller kits get the same effect more cheaply by choosing a face whose open licence already matches their reuse needs.
6. **Graphic language.** Component-first: the fetched page's navigation enumerates 37 reusable, accessible components — from Accordion, Back link, Button, Checkboxes and Date input through Error summary, Skip link, Summary list, Tabs, Task list, Text input and Warning text — plus 30 documented patterns grouped as "Ask users for…", "Help users to…" and full-page templates (all nav tallies, read from the fetched page). The graphic language is therefore carried by *structure* (forms, tables, panels) rather than illustration or imagery. A design language can be legible with no decorative layer at all, provided components are rigorous.
7. **Layout conventions.** Dedicated pages for Page template, Layout, Spacing and Section break define a shared skeleton; service pages look alike because the template is shared, not because teams voluntarily converge. The Error summary and Task list components even standardise *behavioural* layout — where errors appear and how progress is communicated.
8. **How the identity is applied.** Teams are directed to the Government Design Principles and the GOV.UK Service Manual, then assemble services from the component library. Application is composition under rules, not free expression — the inverse of campaign-led brands like Spotify, and a reminder that application model must follow communication goal.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** Governance made explicit: a maintainer team at GDS, a public roadmap with a 12-month horizon, published contribution criteria ("Propose a component or pattern", "Develop a component or pattern"), component lifecycle statuses, a Code of Conduct, community co-design and Design System Day events, and versioned releases (GOV.UK Frontend v6.6.0, released 15 October 2026, per the fetched page). The fetched page also records a brand refresh beginning June 2025 with coordinated Frontend releases — consistency maintenance even during change. Boundary: governance of this weight pays off only above a handful of contributing teams; a three-person project would spend more time governing than building, and should instead fix invariants and use templates (the Guardian's model, 4.10.9).
10. **What can be learned.** (a) Publish the *entry criteria* for a component, not just the component — it keeps the library coherent while still accepting contributions. (b) Lifecycle statuses turn drift into a managed pipeline. (c) A shared page template buys more cross-product consistency than any amount of exhortation. (d) An accessibility strategy published as a first-class page signals that it is a requirement, not a feature — see [Accessibility testing](../accessibility/accessibility-testing.md). (e) A dated public roadmap converts consistency from a one-off project into a maintained service.

### 4.2 US Web Design System (USWDS, GSA) — public design system

**Evidence base:** the USWDS homepage was fetched and read this session (`designsystem.digital.gov`, accessed 2026-11-24, and re-fetched for confirmation later the same day). Typeface names, token naming and colour values are on linked pages that were not fetched.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** Its tagline is "The design system for the federal government"; its mission statement is "We make it easier to build accessible, mobile-friendly government websites". The message is usability and accessibility as federal obligations, delivered as infrastructure. Where GOV.UK leads with consistency, USWDS leads with accessibility — the same institutional goal, a different emphasised value, visible from the first line of the fetched page.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Competent, service-oriented, engineering-flavoured — less editorial than GOV.UK, more of a toolkit. The homepage's dominant content is a browsable inventory of the toolkit: the navigation tallies 47 component pages, 3 pattern groups, 5 templates, 8 design-token categories and 15 utilities (nav counts; the page itself states no numeric totals). A system that presents itself as a walkable inventory is communicating engineering transparency.
3. **Logo system.** Agency identities remain separate; USWDS positions itself as shared infrastructure beneath them. The lesson for multi-brand organisations: the system can serve the *whole estate* without owning the brands (compare [Brand architecture](../branding/brand-architecture.md)). A design system and a brand system are different layers that must coexist without merging — and the case shows the layering works because the system claims only *implementation* consistency, leaving identity claims to each agency.
4. **Colour system.** Colour is published as design tokens with an accessibility target (token page linked, not fetched). *General knowledge*: USWDS token families are named by colour family and numeric grade (for example `primary-600`), a naming scheme that encodes lightness steps and makes accessible pairings mechanical — verify against the live colour page before reuse. Graded naming is what turns contrast rules ([Colour accessibility](../colour/colour-accessibility.md)) into a lookup rather than a test, and it composes: two graded families can be paired by rule where arbitrary palettes must be paired by eye.
5. **Typography.** The fetched page names no typefaces; it links to Typography and a Font size and family utility. *General knowledge*: USWDS ships with Public Sans, an open-source face commissioned for government use, plus a serif companion for editorial contexts — verify before citing. The pattern (commission an open-licensed face for institutional use) recurs in this file: USWDS, Ubuntu and IBM Carbon all made type free to use by their communities.
6. **Graphic language.** Institutional-plain, with data visualisation treated as a first-class component category. Imagery plays a minor role; the voice is carried by structure, type and tokenised colour. Note the explicit presence of "Validation" and "Character count" as components: in a service context, *feedback mechanics* are part of the visual language.
7. **Layout conventions.** A Grid component plus five full-page templates (404, documentation, landing, authentication, form) standardise page skeletons, each promising "UX, accessibility, and implementation guidance". Bundling three kinds of guidance per component — experience, accessibility, implementation — is a documentation pattern worth copying ([Design system documentation](../design-systems/documentation.md)).
8. **How the identity is applied.** Agencies adopt the system as a package: token categories, utilities, components, patterns and templates, then customise within the token space. Adoption breadth is published — "dozens of agencies and nearly 200 sites" — a consistency metric in itself: the system measures and advertises its own coverage.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** Open-source maintenance on GitHub (`uswds/uswds`), an "active open source community of government engineers, content specialists, and designers", versioned releases (v3.14.0 at fetch time) with migration guides for 3.0 and 2.0, and a dedicated accessibility documentation section. The migration guides matter most: they are what let a system evolve for years without fragmenting into per-agency forks.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) Token categories + utilities + components + patterns + templates is a complete five-layer inventory model for any system — see [Component systems](../design-systems/component-systems.md). (b) Publishing adoption breadth ("nearly 200 sites") is governance feedback: it makes the payoff of consistency visible. (c) Migration guides between major versions are what let a system evolve without forking — the same discipline a brand kit needs when it changes platform. (d) Accessibility named in the mission statement, not just the docs, is the strongest positioning an institutional system can adopt.

### 4.3 MIT brand system — university identity

**Evidence base:** the MIT brand site's "Do/Don't" page was fetched and read this session (`brand.mit.edu/applying-brand/do-dont`, accessed 2026-11-24; the first fetch attempt timed out and the immediate retry succeeded). This page is unusually rich in enforceable numbers and is the single best evidence base in this file; its rules are authoritative for MIT but are MIT's own conventions, not general law.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** The page's emphasis is consistency, precision and "protecting mark integrity"; it explicitly calls out "visual confusion" between logos as a risk. An institute at global scale is communicating one identity while hundreds of departments, labs and merchandise vendors touch the brand. The do/don't page exists because that scale produces exactly the confusions it lists.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Confident and institutional, with a controlled playful note — the mascot rule "Don't change Tim's fur colour. He likes to keep it brown." The personality reading: engineering rigour with room for exactly one sanctioned warm element. One licensed point of play, everything else strict, is a personality-management technique in itself.
3. **Logo system.** A defined family: the updated MIT logo; lock-ups in one-line and three-line name configurations; the MIT seal; sub-brand logos issued via the Institute Office of Communications; endorsed department logos carrying a distinct symbol with "MIT" letters in a complementary custom font; and three sanctioned social-media icon options, with retired designs explicitly prohibited. The logo is a standalone graphic: never redrawn, never enclosed in another shape, never edited or "deconstructed". Two eligibility rules deserve note. First, sub-branding is available only to departments, labs, centres, institutes, initiatives and programmes — the fetched page explicitly excludes publications, events, resources, external organisations and student groups from sub-brand treatment. Second, endorsed branding carries placement rules: the MIT logo sits at the top of web pages and in the visual field of the department name on print, never hidden at page bottoms or back covers, and plain text is used for "MIT" alongside department names rather than fusing the logo into a new identity. The event exclusion matters for this repository's core use case: at institutional scale, events are *consumers* of the brand, not holders of it — while section 4.11 shows the inverse where the event is itself the brand.
4. **Colour system.** Single-colour logo rule ("two or more colors on the logo are prohibited"); the seal renders only in "MIT red, black, or white"; merchandise may use other colours but collateral uses core colours only. Social media places the logo on palette colours except black/white (reserved for flagship channels) and excludes greys because they "may appear as black or white on some screens" — a rare example of a rule written for *screen rendering behaviour*, not print intent.
5. **Typography.** The Institute name is set in Neue Haas Grotesk Medium "in no other typeface"; merchandise uses "Collegiate Style/Athletic Block" for the acronym; the year 1861 must use normal type treatment. Two faces, two roles, both named exactly — a minimal role contract of the kind recommended in [Typeface selection](../typography/typeface-selection.md). Naming the weight ("Medium") as well as the family closes the last degree of freedom that usually causes drift.
6. **Graphic language.** Minimal: logo, seal, mascot, type. The seal has its own integrity rules (no distressed/vintage treatment, no type over it, never paired with the logo), which shows the organisation treating graphic assets like legal instruments. The graphic language is almost entirely *assets* rather than *styles* — coherence is carried by the assets' fixed rules.
7. **Layout conventions.** Numeric clear-space rules: at least 1 inch between the logo and any other graphic or text on merchandise, more at larger sizes; in the acronym-plus-seal combination the letters sit above the seal at 30–40% larger than the seal with at least 1 inch between all elements. Numbers convert an aesthetic argument ("give it room") into a checkable rule — the difference between a rule that governs and a rule that gets argued about. Boundary: the stated figures are floors, not targets — the fetched page itself concedes "more may be needed for larger sizes", so an agent should treat 1 inch as the minimum to enforce and scale it optically above display size.
8. **How the identity is applied.** Via do/don't rules scoped by surface (collateral, merchandise, social media, sub-brands), each with its own tolerances — the same surface-scoped approach an agent should take when extending an event logo across [applications](../applications/posters.md). The page also shows application scope by audience: internal communicators, vendors and social-media managers each get the rules relevant to their outputs.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** Central issuance (sub-brand logos only via the Office of Communications), prohibition of retired assets, exact named typefaces, numeric tolerances, and a single contact point (`mit-brand@mit.edu`) for exceptions. The exception channel is part of the system: predictable escalation prevents well-meaning improvisation.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) One colour + one typeface + numeric clear space is a complete, enforceable minimal kit. (b) Writing rules per surface ("collateral vs merchandise vs social") beats global rules, because misuse is surface-specific. (c) Banning greys near the logo shows the right reflex: design rules should anticipate rendering, not just intent. (d) Retired assets must be banned explicitly or they persist for years — a governance rule in [Brand governance](../branding/brand-governance.md). (e) An endorsed-logo tier (distinct symbol + standardised name component) is the reusable answer when many sub-units need identity without fragmenting the parent brand.

### 4.4 Uber — technology company identity

**Evidence base:** the repository cites Uber's public brand user guide (`assets.uber.com/d/k4nuxdZ8MC7E/user-guide`, cited in [Logo systems](../branding/logo-systems.md)); that page was not fetched this session. The analysis combines that citation with general professional knowledge of the widely documented 2016 rebrand — every claim below should be re-verified against the live guide before reuse.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** A technology platform connecting many products (rides, delivery, freight) that must read as one company; the identity must scale from an app icon to vehicle decals to partner communications. The communication problem is breadth: one brand, many contexts, many third parties.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Engineered, confident, contemporary — the 2016 rebrand was widely read as a move from a scrappy start-up look to a deliberate corporate system. Personality here was rebuilt *as a system*, which is why the case is instructive: the change was architectural (type, rules, colour architecture) rather than a new mark alone.
3. **Logo system.** The "Uber" wordmark as the primary mark, with app-badge and product-level lock-ups managed centrally; the user guide's structure (a top-level user guide artefact) reflects a house-of-sub-products problem solved with lock-up rules ([Brand architecture](../branding/brand-architecture.md)). Sub-products are named under the master brand rather than given independent identities — the branded-house choice, made visible in the guide's structure.
4. **Colour system.** A black-and-white core with mobility-category colours permitted in defined contexts (*general knowledge*: the guide restricts where product colours may appear relative to the core black-and-white identity). The mechanism to copy: a two-layer palette — an invariant core plus licensed per-context colours. The core carries recognition; the licensed layer carries product navigation. Boundary: the licensed layer must stay *licensed* — the moment product colours appear in contexts the core has not sanctioned, the architecture degrades into inconsistency, which is why the partner rules (below) matter as much as the palette itself.
5. **Typography.** Uber commissioned a proprietary type family (Uber Move) for exclusive brand use (*general knowledge*; verify against the live guide). Custom type is the most expensive but most ownable typographic choice — the contrast with MIT's licensed Neue Haas Grotesk is instructive: both buy exclusivity, at different price points. Commissioned type also solves the licensing-audit problem: one owner, clear rights, no per-use negotiation. Its cost is rarely the licence fee but the obligation to keep the family maintained — weights, scripts and screen hinting — which only sustained scale justifies.
6. **Graphic language.** Illustration and photography with geometric, movement-themed motifs (*general knowledge*). The graphic language reinforces "motion" — language derived from the product's meaning, not decoration. This is the graphic-language rule in [Graphic language](../graphic-design/graphic-language.md) applied at scale: the motif vocabulary encodes what the company does.
7. **Layout conventions.** Generous clear space, flat black-and-white layouts with colour as accent (*general knowledge*; the layout specifics live in the guide). The flat, monochrome-first layout is what makes the licensed colour layer legible: restraint in the base creates contrast budget for the accents.
8. **How the identity is applied.** A public, self-serve brand user guide covering co-marketing: the repository also cites Uber Eats' merchant co-marketing page (`merchants.ubereats.com`, cited in several branding files), which shows a second application layer — rules for *partners*, not just employees. Two guide depths (full brand book vs partner do/don't page) is a transferable structure: partners need less than employees, and giving them too much invites misuse.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** Central asset distribution (assets.uber.com serves the canonical files), proprietary type, and partner-facing rules that define what third parties may and may not do with lock-ups and colours. Distribution infrastructure — a live asset portal rather than emailed files — is the quiet engine of the whole system.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) Distribute canonical asset URLs, not files — a live asset portal prevents stale logos the way a package registry prevents stale dependencies. (b) A separate partner layer of the brand guide is needed the moment external organisations may co-brand. (c) Colour can be delegated to contexts only after a strong monochrome core is fixed. (d) Sub-product naming under a master wordmark keeps breadth from becoming fragmentation.

### 4.5 Spotify — technology company identity

**Evidence base:** the repository cites Spotify's newsroom media kit (`newsroom.spotify.com/media-kit/logo-and-brand-assets`, cited in [Brand guidelines](../branding/brand-guidelines.md) and [Visual identity](../branding/visual-identity.md)); it was not fetched this session. Analysis combines that citation with widely documented general knowledge — verify details against the live media kit before reuse.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** A consumer entertainment brand whose identity must survive in tiny app icons, loud campaign imagery and third-party editorial coverage; the media kit exists so journalists and partners render the brand correctly *without help*. The communication problem is opposite to MIT's: less about control under scrutiny, more about standing out in feeds and shelves.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Playful, energetic, contemporary-pop; the widely imitated duotone imagery treatment reads as musical energy rather than corporate neutrality. The personality is carried as much by the *treatment* (duotone) as by the palette — a reminder that imagery treatment is a brand voice, not a filter ([Image treatment](../imagery/image-treatment.md)).
3. **Logo system.** The circular icon and the wordmark as separate assets with defined lock-ups; the media kit distributes both with usage rules. A key structural lesson: the *icon* and the *name* are independent assets because they must work at different sizes and in different slots (avatar circles and favicons versus footers and invoices). The separation also decouples failure modes: a cramped wordmark does not force the icon out of its slots, and vice versa.
4. **Colour system.** A signature green as the single dominant brand colour, with the duotone treatment deriving palettes from imagery (*general knowledge*). One dominant colour plus an imagery-derived accent system is a strong, low-maintenance palette architecture — see [Palette development](../colour/palette-development.md). Contrast duties in UI are handled by neutrals, freeing the brand colour from functional work.
5. **Typography.** Spotify commissioned a proprietary family (Spotify Sans) for brand and product use (*general knowledge; verify*). Like Uber, Spotify treats exclusive type as an ownable asset — consistent with the distinctive-assets doctrine in Byron Sharp's *Building Distinctive Assets* [canonical]: assets are valuable when competitors cannot use them.
6. **Graphic language.** Duotone photography, bold type and saturated colour fields; the language is imagery-led, the inverse of GOV.UK's structure-led system. Both are valid; the choice follows the brand's job (energy vs trust). Note also that the duotone treatment is a *rule*, reproducible by any photographer or designer given source images — that reproducibility is what makes it a system element rather than a style.
7. **Layout conventions.** Campaign layouts give type and logo generous protected space over saturated imagery (*general knowledge*). Protecting the mark's contrast zone over busy imagery is the layout rule that imagery-led brands most often need to state explicitly.
8. **How the identity is applied.** A public media kit with downloadable logo assets and explicit do/don't guidance — application by distribution: put the correct assets where third parties already look for them. The media kit also handles the *press* context explicitly (newsroom), showing awareness that editorial use has different needs from partner use.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** One dominant colour, one commissioned type family, one photographic treatment, and a single authoritative distribution point (the media kit). Four decisions, heavily enforced, outperform forty loosely enforced ones — the recurring arithmetic of distinctiveness.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) A media kit is brand governance for the outside world; every event or organisation expecting press coverage needs one, however small. (b) Distinctiveness can be bought with one decisive colour plus one imagery treatment rather than a complex palette. (c) The icon/wordmark split anticipates every real placement problem (favicons, avatar circles, print footers). (d) An imagery treatment that is defined as a reproducible rule converts every future image into a brand asset.

### 4.6 Material Design — product design system with token architecture

**Evidence base:** the repository cites `m3.material.io/foundations/design-tokens` and `m2.material.io/design/iconography/system-icons.html` ([Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md), [Iconography](../graphic-design/iconography.md)). A fetch attempt of the M3 tokens page this session returned no page body (the site is JavaScript-rendered), so nothing here comes from that fetch. The analysis rests on general professional knowledge of the published system — verify against m3.material.io before reuse.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** Google communicates that thousands of apps across Android and web can feel coherent while remaining individually themed; Material is identity-as-platform, the largest-scale test of component and token thinking. Its communication goal is *coherence of behaviour* more than uniformity of appearance — apps should move, respond and be organised the same way.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Systematic, engineered, optimistic; Material 3 (Material You) explicitly foregrounds personalisation over fixed aesthetics. A system confident enough to let users recolour the UI is a system whose real invariants are structural (motion, shape, spacing), not chromatic.
3. **Logo system.** Material's own identity is secondary to its role as a system; the transferable artefact is not its logo but its architecture. The case demonstrates that a "brand" for a platform can consist of behaviour standards plus tokens, with the visual mark almost incidental.
4. **Colour system.** *General knowledge*: Material 3 organises colour as roles — primary, on-primary, secondary, tertiary, surface, on-surface, error and so on — generated into a full scheme from a single seed colour, with "on-" roles making accessible text-on-colour pairings structural rather than manual (see [Colour accessibility](../colour/colour-accessibility.md)). Dynamic colour remaps the roles per user wallpaper: role-based colour architecture enables personalisation without anarchy.
5. **Typography.** *General knowledge*: the M3 type scale defines named roles (display, headline, title, body, label, each in large/medium/small variants), giving every text element a role instead of a raw size — the same role contract recommended in [Typographic hierarchy](../typography/typographic-hierarchy.md). Fifteen named roles cover nearly every UI text need; teams stop inventing sizes and start assigning roles. The boundary is long-form text: roles are tuned for interface strings, so editorial or document contexts built on Material still need a supplementary reading-scale decision ([Editorial design](../typography/editorial-design.md)).
6. **Graphic language.** System icons with defined stroke conventions, tonal elevation (colour shifts rather than hard shadows), and shape scales with defined corner radii (*general knowledge*). Defining shape as a scale (small/medium/large corner radii) turns a taste question into a three-choice lookup.
7. **Layout conventions.** An 8dp spacing convention with a responsive grid and documented elevation levels (*general knowledge; verify*). A single base unit (8) with a small set of approved increments is the spacing equivalent of graded colour tokens.
8. **How the identity is applied.** Teams build from published components and theme them through tokens; theme files — not hand-edited components — carry the identity. Application is thereby reversible: replacing a theme replaces the look without touching the components.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** A three-tier token model (*general knowledge*: reference tokens hold raw values; system tokens bind roles to references; component tokens bind component parts to system tokens), which localises change: re-theming touches only the reference tier. Consistency is enforced by *data structure*: the wiring makes deviations harder than compliance.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) Role naming ("on-primary") encodes accessibility into vocabulary — the cheapest consistency mechanism in this file. (b) A seeded-generation scheme shows how to offer palette variation to many stakeholders while keeping relationships fixed. (c) Tier separation in tokens is what makes a system themable at scale — the direct model for [Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md). (d) When personalisation is a goal, move the invariants from colour into motion, shape and structure.

### 4.7 Atlassian Design System — product design system

**Evidence base:** the repository cites `atlassian.design/foundations`, `/foundations/illustrations` and `/foundations/tokens` ([Graphic language](../graphic-design/graphic-language.md), [Illustration](../graphic-design/illustration.md), [Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md)); none were fetched this session. Analysis from those citations plus general professional knowledge.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** Enterprise collaboration software for work tools (Jira, Confluence); the identity must feel professional, human and calm across dozens of products and a marketplace of third-party apps. The communication problem is density: tools people use all day must not fatigue them.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Friendly-professional: the widely noted investment in illustration humanises otherwise dense tooling. Warmth is delivered through a *medium* (illustration) rather than through colour or type — keeping the functional surface calm.
3. **Logo system.** Product logos sit under a coherent parent identity with a defined relationship — an endorsed architecture executed inside a design system ([Brand architecture](../branding/brand-architecture.md)). The design system and the brand architecture reinforce each other: shared components make endorsed logos easier to keep coherent.
4. **Colour system.** Foundations include colour tokens with accessibility guidance (*cited pages, not fetched*); *general knowledge*: Atlassian publishes brand colours and functional colours (links, errors, warnings) as separate concerns. Separating brand colour from functional colour is what keeps UI semantics stable while campaigns vary. The accessibility guidance attached to the tokens is the part to copy: a colour that cannot pass its intended role's contrast requirement should fail at token review, not in a shipped screen.
5. **Typography.** Charlie Sans, a proprietary family across the suite (*general knowledge; verify*). One family across a dozen products is the single-family strategy ([Font pairing](../typography/font-pairing.md) 3.4) applied organisation-wide.
6. **Graphic language.** The distinctive element is illustration: the foundations' illustrations page (cited in the repository) documents style rules so dozens of illustrators produce coherent work — the strongest public example that illustration needs a *system*, not just a style (see [Illustration](../graphic-design/illustration.md)). The page's existence proves the failure it prevents: without documented rules, illustration is the first layer of a brand to fragment.
7. **Layout conventions.** Spacing and grid conventions published within foundations (*general knowledge*). Publishing them under "foundations" — before components — frames spacing as shared vocabulary rather than per-product choice.
8. **How the identity is applied.** Product teams consume foundations, components and patterns; third-party app developers get published tokens so marketplace apps inherit the look — application to *external developers* is the notable extension beyond internal teams. Brand control reaching outside the org chart is rare and deliberate: it required machine-readable tokens rather than PDF guidelines.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** The foundations layer (colour, iconography, illustration, typography, tokens) sits beneath all products; tokens make values machine-checkable rather than convention-dependent. Machine-checkability is the qualitative leap from document-based to pipeline-based governance.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) Illustration style guides are a necessary part of a kit the moment more than one illustrator works on it. (b) Publishing tokens to external developers extends brand control to a boundary where contracts cannot easily reach. (c) "Foundations" as the top-level concept — values before components — is a documentation architecture worth copying in [Design system documentation](../design-systems/documentation.md). (d) Brand colour and functional colour need separate governance.

### 4.8 IBM Carbon — product design system with open-source type

**Evidence base:** the repository cites the Carbon Design System (`https://www.carbondesignsystem.com`, cited in [Illustration](../graphic-design/illustration.md)); the site was not fetched this session. The analysis rests on widely documented general professional knowledge — including IBM's long-public design language — and every specific below should be verified against the live Carbon site before reuse.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** IBM communicates its engineering heritage and scale: one design system serving a large portfolio of enterprise products, under a corporate identity with a century of history. The communication problem is continuity — modernising without erasing.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Systematic, precise, confident; the personality is inseparable from IBM's design heritage, most visibly Paul Rand's 8-bar logo of 1972 [canonical — widely documented design history; do not cite specific dates beyond this without verification]. A fifty-year-old mark operating inside a modern token system is the case's central fact.
3. **Logo system.** The 8-bar wordmark is used as an intact asset with managed clear space and placement rules; product brands and sub-brands operate under corporate-identity governance (*general knowledge; verify on the live site*). The lesson: an old mark survives platform changes when it is treated as a fixed asset consumed by the system, never redrawn per product.
4. **Colour system.** *General knowledge*: Carbon pairs "productive" colour (a restrained, high-contrast set for UI) with "expressive" colour (a wider set for marketing and brand moments), published as graded tokens with accessibility guidance. The productive/expressive split is the same two-layer architecture Uber uses (invariant core + licensed variation), named explicitly — worth copying wherever UI and campaign work share a brand.
5. **Typography.** IBM Plex, commissioned for IBM and released as open source, with sans, serif and mono variants serving product, document and code contexts (*general knowledge; verify current status*). Plex is the file's most instructive typeface decision: commissioned like Uber Move, but licensed openly like Ubuntu's family — exclusivity achieved through brand context rather than through legal restriction.
6. **Graphic language.** A documented pictogram library, data-visualisation guidance and motion patterns, published alongside components (*general knowledge; verify*). Treating pictograms and dataviz as first-class citizens of the graphic language fits an enterprise whose products are dense with data.
7. **Layout conventions.** *General knowledge*: Carbon documents a grid built on 2x/8px increments, aligning spacing decisions across products. As with Material, a single base unit plus graded increments keeps dozens of product teams on one rhythm.
8. **How the identity is applied.** Components, tokens and patterns are published open source; teams inside and outside IBM build on the same library, with governance for contributions. Open-sourcing the *system* (not just the type) extends the Atlassian lesson: external adoption is only governable when the assets are versioned and inspectable.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** Token architecture, versioned open-source releases, and heritage discipline around the mark; consistency spans both the digital layer (tokens) and the historical layer (the protected 8-bar mark). The system demonstrates that "brand consistency" can hold across fifty years of implementation technology.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) Heritage marks and modern token systems are compatible — fix the mark, expose the values. (b) The productive/expressive colour split separates UI discipline from campaign energy without two brands. (c) Open-licensed commissioned type buys goodwill and ecosystem adoption while remaining exclusive in practice. (d) Pictogram and dataviz libraries deserve the same governance as components in data-heavy brands.

### 4.9 Ubuntu — open-source identity

**Evidence base:** no Ubuntu brand page was fetched this session; no repository file cites one. This case rests entirely on general professional knowledge of a long-established public identity and is marked accordingly — treat every specific below as a hypothesis to verify against Canonical's published brand resources before reuse.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** Canonical communicates that a community-built operating system can carry a coherent, recognisable identity across thousands of independent contributors, flavours and derivatives. The communication problem is adoption: the identity must survive people who cannot be reviewed, trained or contracted.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Human, precise, quietly distinctive — an engineering brand that deliberately avoids corporate gloss. The personality survives because the rules are few and the assets are free: nothing about the identity depends on enforcement capacity the community does not have.
3. **Logo system.** The "Circle of Friends" emblem plus the Ubuntu wordmark; the emblem works without the name, which is why it survives in community-made artefacts. Separate emblem and wordmark, each usable alone with rules, is the same split Spotify makes — it appears to be a general pattern for community-scale marks. The emblem-first order is deliberate: in improvised contexts (forum avatars, stickers, slides by volunteers) the symbol appears far more often than the full lock-up, so the symbol must carry the identity alone.
4. **Colour system.** A signature orange with aubergine/neutral supporting tones (*general knowledge*). One assertive brand colour over neutral structure recurs across this file (Spotify, Ubuntu) and appears to be a reliable pattern for community-adopted identities — assertive enough to be recognised in improvised contexts, simple enough to be reproduced without a style guide at hand.
5. **Typography.** The Ubuntu type family was commissioned for the brand and released under a free licence so every community member can use it legally — the inverse of Uber's proprietary Move: both commissioned families, opposite licensing strategies, each achieving exclusivity in its own ecosystem (Ubuntu's by licence terms in free culture, Uber's by ownership). Verify the current licence before relying on this.
6. **Graphic language.** The "circle of friends" motif and a flat, geometric visual language established in the widely documented 2010 rebrand (specific agency attributions are not verified here; do not cite them without checking). Flatness also has a practical dividend in this context: a flat mark survives reproduction on wiki pages, terminal art, stickers and low-quality print.
7. **Layout conventions.** Documentation-heavy contexts (wikis, release notes, forums) adopt shared templates rather than bespoke layouts — consistency achieved through constrained environments, similar to GOV.UK's template strategy. When volunteers produce most artefacts, the *environment* is the enforcer.
8. **How the identity is applied.** Published brand resources plus licence terms; derivatives (flavours) follow variant rules rather than redesigns. Flavour branding is the community equivalent of the endorsed-logo tier (MIT 4.3.3): variation within a designed slot.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** Free licensing of the core assets removes the practical excuse for off-brand substitutes — the most important governance insight in this file: *if you want people to comply, make the compliant path the easiest one*.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) Licence design is brand governance: free-for-community licensing buys consistency money cannot. (b) A brand for volunteers must assume no central review; the kit itself has to do the policing. (c) Commissioned-but-open type is a viable middle path between proprietary type and default system fonts — relevant whenever an event or community brand needs broad reuse rights (compare [Typeface selection](../typography/typeface-selection.md) and [Font pairing](../typography/font-pairing.md)). (d) Simple, flat, high-contrast assets are the most robust under uncontrolled reproduction.

### 4.10 The Guardian — editorial identity

**Evidence base:** no Guardian guideline page was fetched this session and none is cited in the repository. This case rests entirely on widely documented general professional knowledge of a heavily published editorial identity; every specific below must be verified against current Guardian brand resources before reuse. It is included because the mission lists editorial systems as a priority and no editorial page could be fetched — treat it as a structural hypothesis, not a sourced account.

1. **What the organisation communicates.** Independent journalism with a distinctive editorial voice. The communication problem is unique to editorial: the identity must survive *daily production at volume* — hundreds of pages and thousands of articles a year — while still expressing a personality strong enough to distinguish the brand in a crowded news market.
2. **Apparent brand personality.** Liberal, witty, self-aware; the Guardian is widely noted as one of the few news brands whose design has a recognisable sense of humour. The lesson in that: personality in an institutional brand can live in type voice and layout rhythm rather than in logo gymnastics.
3. **Logo system.** A masthead-as-logo approach: the newspaper's name set in its own typefaces *is* the mark, with print and digital masthead variants coordinated centrally (*general knowledge; verify current masthead forms*). Editorial brands demonstrate the limiting case of logo systems — when the product is text, the logotype and the product's voice are the same asset, and the usual question of "which variant goes where" becomes a question about the typography itself.
4. **Colour system.** A signature deep blue as the core brand colour, with print sections historically distinguished by colour-coded bands and supplements rather than by decorative colour (*general knowledge; verify*). Colour in editorial settings is navigational first: it tells the reader where they are in the paper — a role model for any content-heavy kit ([Palette development](../colour/palette-development.md)).
5. **Typography.** A commissioned-family lineage: the widely documented Guardian Egyptian (2005) through the current digital faces for headline and text (*typeface names and attribution to its designers are canonical type-design history; verify the current family names in use*). The enduring lesson: a bespoke type voice is the single most ownable asset in text-dense media — everything else in the page is grid, which many publications share.
6. **Graphic language.** Typography-led, with photography as the main imagery and restrained decorative devices. In text-dense media the graphic language is mostly *typographic behaviour* — scale steps, rule weights, spacing rhythm — governed by editorial conventions rather than visual fashion ([Editorial design](../typography/editorial-design.md)).
7. **Layout conventions.** A modular grid with a strong lead-versus-secondary hierarchy, applied consistently across print and digital so the newspaper's structure reads the same in both media (*general knowledge; verify*). The digital layout echoes the paper's structure instead of imitating portal conventions — a deliberate identity choice visible on every page. For an agent, the transferable point is that layout conventions in text-dense media are mostly *hierarchy conventions*: which stories lead, how secondary material retreats — the province of [Typographic hierarchy](../typography/typographic-hierarchy.md) and [Information hierarchy](../information-design/information-hierarchy.md) more than of grids alone.
8. **How the identity is applied.** Through production: page templates, styles and the sub-editing process carry the rules, because nightly deadlines make committee review impossible. Application is embedded in the workflow — the most robust enforcement model of any case in this file, and the reason editorial identities stay coherent at volumes no other sector reaches.
9. **What makes the system consistent.** The type family, the blue, the masthead and the template library; enforcement is process-embedded (templates and editorial workflow) rather than a separate brand-police function. Consistency here is a *production consequence*, not a review outcome.
10. **What can be learned.** (a) In text-dense media, type is the identity — commission it, or if that is impossible, choose a distinctive licence-clean face ([Typeface selection](../typography/typeface-selection.md)). (b) Daily production volume forces systemisation: templates are the enforcement layer, the same insight GOV.UK reaches from the opposite direction. (c) One brand colour plus one typographic voice is sufficient identity for even the most content-heavy brand. (d) Wherever deadlines rule out review, move enforcement into templates and tooling — a rule for any fast-turnaround kit, including events. Boundary: the Guardian's model assumes a professional production staff; a volunteer team inherits the templates-and-tools part but not the sub-editing discipline, so its templates must be harder to break (locked colours, fixed type styles) than an newsroom's.

### 4.11 Event branding (the Olympic pattern) — verified leads, not a full case

**Evidence base:** no event-brand page could be fetched this session — a fetch attempt of an Olympic design-archive page timed out, and no other event page was reachable within the session's research budget. What follows rests on three sources verified to exist as search results this session (see References); it records leads and structural inferences, not page content. Every specific inside those documents is unverified until one is fetched.

The verified leads form a coherent picture of how a mega-event identity is governed. The International Olympic Committee publishes an Olympic Brand Guidelines manual of roughly 138 pages, described in its search-record snippet as intended "to help bring the Olympic brand to life — a visual identity inspired by our heritage"; an Olympic design archive (theolympicdesign.com) maintains per-Games "Look of the Games" documentation, including a Milano Cortina 2026 page describing a visual identity that "celebrates the beauty and spirit of Italy"; and a National Olympic Committee publishes dated third-party brand rules ("Guidelines for brands" under Rule 40) that define a blackout period around Milano Cortina 2026 of 30 January – 24 February 2026, during which non-sponsor brands may not use Games imagery. Three artefacts, three governance layers: a manual for the rights holder, an archive of the applied identity, and a calendar-enforced co-brand regime for everyone else.

Three lessons survive without page access, because they are inferences from what *exists*, not from what the pages say. First, a mega-event identity ships as manual + archive + co-brand regime — the same invariant-core/licensed-variation split of section 5.1, with the licensed variation here governed by *contracts and dates* rather than by tokens. Second, event identity is the limiting case of time-boxed consistency: the blackout window shows enforcement running on a calendar, where MIT's rules (4.3) run permanently — an event kit therefore needs explicit start and end dates on every rule, including its own. Third, per-Games archiving treats each identity as a designed artefact worth preserving after expiry — the same retired-assets discipline MIT enforces by prohibition (4.3.9), practised here by curation. Combined with MIT's exclusion of events from sub-brand eligibility, these leads bracket the event-identity problem from both sides: institutions treat events as brand *consumers*; mega-events treat themselves as the brand other organisations must orbit.

The boundary is as important as the pattern: the emblem concept, palette and typefaces of Milano Cortina 2026 are deliberately not recorded here, because the page that would supply them could not be read. Treat this section as a to-fetch list for the next agent — the three URLs in the References are the starting points — and see [Fixed logo analysis](../branding/fixed-logo-analysis.md) for the entry-point analysis a fixed event mark needs, and [Event collateral](../applications/event-collateral.md) for the application layer a fetched case would inform.

## 5. Cross-cutting lessons

Synthesis of the ten cases — this section is the repository's own analysis, not attributable to any single source. Each lesson names both the mechanism and the constraint that makes it work, because a mechanism stripped of its constraint becomes superstition: teams copy the practice, keep none of its force, and cannot say when it stops applying.

### 5.1 Every durable system separates an invariant core from licensed variation

MIT fixes logo colour and type absolutely but delegates merchandise colour; Uber fixes a black-and-white core and licenses product colours; IBM Carbon names the split "productive vs expressive"; Material fixes colour *roles* and remaps the values; Ubuntu fixes the mark and licenses the type freely. The recurring architecture is a core that never varies plus a defined space where variation is allowed, each with its own rules. A brand-kit agent should design this split deliberately: decide first what must never change, then what may change and under whose approval.

### 5.2 Consistency is produced by distribution and enforcement, not by the design

The mechanisms visible across cases: central asset distribution (Uber, Spotify), contribution criteria and lifecycle statuses (GOV.UK), published adoption metrics (USWDS), surface-scoped do/don't rules with numbers (MIT), free licences that remove excuses (Ubuntu), and token tiers that localise change (Material, Carbon). None of these is an aesthetic decision. The practical implication for [Brand governance](../branding/brand-governance.md) is that a kit is unfinished until its enforcement mechanism exists — and the mechanism must match the contributor population: contracts and review for employees, published tokens for external developers, free licences for volunteers.

### 5.3 Numeric rules appear exactly where rendering misbehaves

MIT's 1-inch clear space, the 30–40% acronym-to-seal ratio, the grey ban justified by screen rendering, USWDS's accessibility-targeted token grades: where a system states a number, it is usually converting a recurring failure into a rule. When building a new kit, the places to expect numbers are clear space, minimum sizes, contrast pairs and the boundary between colour contexts. Everywhere else, qualitative guidance is honest and numbers would be false precision.

### 5.4 One dominant colour plus one imagery treatment is a recurring distinctiveness pattern

Spotify (green + duotone), Ubuntu (orange + geometric motif) and, differently weighted, Uber (black/white + motion imagery) all buy recognitiveness with a single assertive colour and a single consistent treatment rather than a complex palette. MIT buys it with one accent colour (MIT red) used with severity. The pattern suits small organisations and events precisely because it is cheap to enforce: one colour decision, one treatment decision, done — see [Colour psychology](../colour/colour-psychology.md) and [Image treatment](../imagery/image-treatment.md).

### 5.5 Typeface strategy is a spectrum from ownership to openness

The nine cases sort into four typeface postures: licensed-but-exclusive (MIT's Neue Haas Grotesk, named exactly including weight), proprietary-commissioned (Uber Move, Spotify Sans, Atlassian's Charlie Sans), commissioned-then-open-sourced (Ubuntu's family, IBM Plex), and standardised open catalogues (USWDS's open faces, every system built on open font libraries). Exclusivity, not the licence fee, is what each posture buys: Plex is free in law yet exclusive in practice because it is bound to IBM's context. An agent choosing type for a new kit should decide first how much *ownability* the brand needs, then how much reuse rights others need — the two answers pick the posture ([Typeface selection](../typography/typeface-selection.md)).

### 5.8 Event and campaign kits add a time dimension to every invariant

The event leads of section 4.11 expose a dimension the other cases understate: for a time-bounded brand, invariants expire. The Olympic pattern enforces identity on a calendar (a dated blackout window for third-party brands); the Guardian's daily production makes consistency a rhythm rather than a state; and a festival kit's deliverables have lifespans from hours (wristbands) to years (a logo carried across editions). Three consequences transfer to any time-bounded kit. First, every rule should carry its validity dates, so an expired constraint announces itself instead of silently persisting. Second, the retired-assets ban becomes *scheduled expiry* rather than an enforcement action — the kit is designed to retire its own artefacts. Third, an archive is the third governance artefact: the Olympic pattern's per-Games documentation shows that an expired edition stays reusable as reference precisely because it is taken out of circulation. A brand-kit agent building for an event should therefore answer the ten questions twice — once for the live edition, and once for what the kit becomes after it ends.

## 6. Applying the lessons to a new brand kit

A condensed procedure derived from the cases, for the repository's core use case of extending a fixed logo ([Fixed logo analysis](../branding/fixed-logo-analysis.md)):

1. **Fix the invariants first.** Choose the logo colour rule (MIT: single colour), the primary typeface and the clear-space minimum as numbers, not vibes (MIT's 1 inch; scale proportionally for smaller marks — this ratio translation is a convention, verify optically).
2. **Choose the palette architecture.** Either one dominant colour over neutrals (Spotify/Ubuntu pattern) or a role-named functional palette (Material/USWDS pattern); role naming is the safer default for digital products, the dominant-colour pattern for campaign-led brands and events. If both worlds exist in the project, adopt Carbon's productive/expressive split.
3. **Split the mark.** Produce emblem-only and wordmark-only variants plus lock-ups (Spotify, Ubuntu) so every slot — favicon, avatar, footer, badge — has a sanctioned asset ([Logo systems](../branding/logo-systems.md)).
4. **Write do/don't rules per surface.** Enumerate the surfaces (poster, social, web, print, partner use) and give each its own short rules (MIT), including an explicit retired-assets ban. Where rendering is risky — logos over imagery, logos on screens, small sizes — attach numbers.
5. **Publish the assets.** A single canonical location with current files (Uber, Spotify); for open communities, choose licences that make compliance the easiest path (Ubuntu). A media kit counts even at event scale: one page with the logo files and three rules.
6. **Encode tokens where code is involved.** Role-based token names for colour and type (Material, USWDS) so themes can change without breaking relationships ([Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md)).
7. **Decide the enforcement layer.** Contribution criteria if many teams contribute (GOV.UK), a media kit if third parties publish (Spotify), partner rules if co-branding exists (Uber), free licensing if volunteers build (Ubuntu). More than one may apply — but each must have an owner.
8. **Date the rules and plan the archive.** Give every rule its validity window — the Olympic pattern's calendar enforcement scaled down (section 5.8) — and decide where the kit goes when the edition ends: a sealed archive folder with the final rendered artefacts makes the retired-assets ban cost one move instead of one audit.

Steps 1–4 produce the kit's content; steps 5–8 produce its persistence. A kit with only steps 1–4 is a design, not a system.

### 6.1 Worked micro-example: extending a fixed festival logo

To show the procedure at the scale most agents will actually meet, this subsection (the repository's own worked example) walks the seven steps for a hypothetical three-day regional design festival with a fixed logo. Every number below is a labelled convention or an explicit choice — nothing here is inherited from a case's figures.

**The inputs.** A fixed logo: a compact emblem plus wordmark, supplied as vector files; a two-person volunteer team; deliverables spanning posters, social posts, wristbands, a simple website, and sponsor acknowledgements.

1. **Fix the invariants.** Logo renders single-colour (MIT's rule, chosen for cheap reproduction); clear space set at 0.5× the emblem's cap height — a conventional starting point, to be checked optically on the smallest artefact (wristband) and largest (poster); minimum emblem width 24 px on screen — a convention, verify legibly. Typeface: one open-licensed grotesque for everything (single-family strategy, [Font pairing](../typography/font-pairing.md) 3.4) — two volunteers cannot govern a pairing.
2. **Choose the palette architecture.** One dominant festival colour over neutrals (the Spotify/Ubuntu pattern), plus exactly one accessible text pair for the website, verified against WCAG thresholds before commitment ([Colour accessibility](../colour/colour-accessibility.md)). No secondary palette: a two-person team cannot enforce one.
3. **Split the mark.** Four sanctioned assets: emblem only (avatars, favicons, wristbands), wordmark only (footers), horizontal lock-up (posters, web header), vertical lock-up (social). Each exported at a minimum-size and an optimal-size variant ([Logo systems](../branding/logo-systems.md)).
4. **Write do/don't rules per surface.** Five surfaces, five short rule sets in the style of MIT's page: poster (logo on flat colour only, never over imagery without a plate); social (emblem only in avatars; no third-party filters over the mark); print (single colour; the 1-inch-style numeric floor scaled to artefact); web (logo links home; never recoloured in CSS); sponsor use (co-brand rules: sponsor marks separated by a rule, never merged — the Uber partner-layer logic at miniature scale). Include the retired-assets ban: the previous festival's logo files are removed from the asset folder, not just deprecated.
5. **Publish the assets.** One canonical folder with the four assets, the palette values, and the five rule sets — functionally a one-page media kit (Spotify's mechanism scaled down). For a volunteer team, this is also where the free-licence decision lands: assets are published with an explicit reuse licence so other volunteers can build compliantly (Ubuntu's mechanism).
6. **Encode tokens where code is involved.** Five CSS custom properties — two brand colours, three neutrals — plus the font stack, defined once and imported by the site; no hex values anywhere else ([Design tokens](../design-systems/design-tokens.md)). At this scale a token *file* is overkill; the custom properties are the entire pipeline.
7. **Decide the enforcement layer.** Two volunteers: templates do the policing, not review. Poster and social templates are prebuilt in the free tier of a template tool ([Tools and software](tools-and-software.md) section 5) with colours and fonts locked; the website is built from the token file; sponsors receive the co-brand page from step 4. The single exception channel is one named person.

The result is a complete, enforceable kit produced in the pattern of every case above, with each mechanism traceable to a case: MIT's per-surface rules and numeric floors, Spotify's asset split and media kit, Uber's partner layer, Ubuntu's licence-as-governance, GOV.UK's template-first consistency, and Material's role-based colour — all at a scale where every mechanism costs hours, not months.

### 6.2 What this procedure deliberately does not cover

Honest boundaries matter as much as the steps. The procedure above does not cover print production specification (bleed, colour profiles, paper — see [Posters](../applications/posters.md) and the print conventions in the applications files); illustration and photography systems (a single festival usually needs no illustration rulebook — but see [Illustration](../graphic-design/illustration.md) the moment a second illustrator joins); legal review of sponsorship agreements and rights (see [Image rights](../imagery/image-rights.md) for the image-specific layer); and motion or sonic identity, which none of the ten cases required at this scale. It also assumes the logo itself is fixed and usable: if the supplied mark fails legibility, single-colour reproduction or contrast checks, no amount of system-building rescues it — that analysis comes first ([Fixed logo analysis](../branding/fixed-logo-analysis.md)).

## 7. Common mistakes when generalising from case studies

- **Copying artefacts instead of mechanisms.** Adopting a colour value because a famous system uses it ignores that the value was chosen for that brand's context and contrasts; the mechanism (role naming, contrast verification via [Colour accessibility](../colour/colour-accessibility.md)) is what transfers.
- **Reading personality off one page.** A do/don't page shows governance posture, not the whole personality; avoid confident personality claims beyond what the evidence base supports.
- **Importing scale-inappropriate governance.** GOV.UK's contribution pipeline is right for thousands of teams and wrong for an event with two volunteers; scale the enforcement layer to the contributor count.
- **Treating unverified details as facts.** Several cases above rest on general knowledge marked *verify*; reusing such a detail in a deliverable without checking the live source repeats the exact failure the [source-accessibility index](source-accessibility.md) exists to prevent.
- **Ignoring the constraint that produced the choice.** MIT's grey ban exists because of screen rendering; deleting the reason and keeping the rule produces rules nobody can apply. Every lesson in section 5 names its constraint — keep them paired.
- **Assuming the visible layer is the whole system.** The fetched and cited pages show rules, not roadmaps, issue trackers and exception logs; concluding "this system is simple" from its public pages underestimates the machinery behind it.
- **Trusting a general-knowledge case beyond its evidence tier.** The Ubuntu and Guardian profiles rest on marked general knowledge; promoting their specifics (colour values, typeface names) into a deliverable without verification converts a labelled hypothesis into an unattributed fact.
- **Reusing time-bounded rules as permanent ones.** The event pattern of section 4.11 runs enforcement on a calendar; copying a dated rule (a blackout window, an edition's palette) outside its validity period imports an expired constraint. Check whether a rule carries dates before treating it as standing, and re-read section 5.8 before applying any case to an event kit.
- **Confusing a design system with a brand kit.** Material, Carbon and Atlassian are *systems serving a brand* — their token architectures cannot generate the identity decisions (voice, colour, type personality) that the brand layer supplies first; a brand-kit agent borrows their governance, not their sequence.
- **Mapping the range instead of choosing.** The ranges in section 5.7 describe what is possible, not what is advisable for one project; a kit that takes role-named colour *and* a media kit *and* a contribution pipeline because each appears in some case has imported three enforcement systems without one contributor population to justify any of them.

## 8. Evaluation checklist

Before reusing a case-study lesson in your own work:

- [ ] I have identified whether the lesson is an established principle, a professional convention, a context-dependent choice, or one organisation's house style.
- [ ] I can state the *constraint* that produced the practice, not just the practice.
- [ ] For any specific number, colour value, typeface name or licensing claim, I have either verified it against the live source or labelled it as unverified.
- [ ] I am copying a mechanism (naming scheme, rule structure, distribution model) rather than an artefact (a palette, a logo shape).
- [ ] The enforcement layer implied by the lesson matches the scale of contributors in my project.
- [ ] The lesson is recorded in the deliverable with its source and provenance tag per [Source accessibility](source-accessibility.md).
- [ ] The kit I am building answers all ten questions of section 4 for itself — invariants named, application surfaces listed, enforcement mechanism chosen.
- [ ] Where I declined to import a case's practice, I can say why in one sentence (scale, constraint, or cost mismatch) — rejecting a lesson is only defensible when the rejection is reasoned.
- [ ] Any specific figure I reuse carries its access date, and I have re-fetched the evidence base if the fact is version-sensitive (section 3's fifth discipline).
