# Brand fundamentals

## 1. What is it?

Brand fundamentals are the core vocabulary and concepts that every branding decision rests on: what a brand actually is (as opposed to what people casually call it), the difference between what an organisation says about itself and what audiences perceive, and the building blocks — positioning, personality, promise, values, purpose, audience, differentiation, recognition and equity — that an identity system is designed to express. Getting this vocabulary precise is not academic pedantry. Each concept has direct, practical consequences for what a designer makes, what a brand kit contains, and how the finished work is judged.

The most consequential distinction is the one Marty Neumeier drew in *The Brand Gap*: "A brand is not a logo. A brand is not a corporate identity system. It's a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or company." The logo is one owned asset among many; the brand is a perception that lives in the audience's memory and is assembled from every encounter they have ever had — designed or accidental. Wally Olins made a compatible point from the corporate-identity tradition: identity is what the organisation sends out, image is what audiences hold in their heads, and the two are related but never identical.

This file defines each fundamental term and explains the practical consequence of the definition. The companion files in this domain build on it: [Brand strategy](brand-strategy.md) turns these concepts into an actionable plan, [Brand discovery](brand-discovery.md) describes the research that grounds them in a real organisation, and [Brand personality](brand-personality.md) deep-dives one specific concept. How the resulting system is designed is covered in [Visual identity development](visual-identity.md).

## 2. Why does it matter?

Because "brand ≠ logo" changes the shape of the deliverable. If a brand were just a logo, a brand kit would contain one file and some colour swatches. Since a brand is a perception built across touchpoints, a competent brand kit must contain everything that reliably shapes that perception: logo system, colour, typography, graphic language, imagery direction, tone of voice, layout rules and examples of the system applied to real artefacts. The mission procedure for brand kit development (§7.1) follows directly from this: an audit, research, positioning, personality, moodboards, colour, type, imagery, applications, testing, guidelines, governance — not merely "draw a logo".

The second reason is evaluative. When an agent or a reviewer judges design output, the judgement must be against the brand, not against personal taste. Knowing that a brand is a perception tells you to test whether the designed identity is *distinctive, memorable, consistent and appropriate* — perception-level criteria. Knowing that brand equity is a financial and behavioural asset tells you why consistency rules exist at all: inconsistency erodes the accumulated recognition that the equity consists of.

The third reason is communicative. Clients, developers and marketers use these words loosely and inconsistently. A designer who can define the difference between promise, positioning and personality can run a better kickoff meeting, write a better brief and defend decisions in language stakeholders already own.

## 3. Core principles

### 3.1 Brand versus logo

A logo is a mark — a symbol, wordmark or combination — used to identify an organisation. The brand is the accumulated perception (Neumeier's "gut feeling") that audiences hold. The logo is a *trigger* for that perception: seeing the mark recalls everything previously experienced and communicated. This is why a technically well-drawn logo can still fail — if the surrounding experience contradicts what the mark promises, the perception is negative no matter how elegant the mark is.

Practical consequences for design:

- A brand kit extends far beyond the logo file. It must specify the whole sensory and verbal surface of the organisation.
- Logo quality is judged on identification performance — distinctiveness, legibility at small sizes, recognisability in monochrome — not on beauty alone.
- When the logo is already fixed (the common event-brand case: extend an existing event logo into a full kit), the rest of the kit must be designed *from* the logo: extracting its geometry, weight, colour temperature and era to build a system that visibly belongs to it. The logo constrains the system; it does not disappear into it.

### 3.2 Brand identity versus brand image

Identity is what the organisation deliberately projects — the designed output: name, mark, palette, typography, voice, behaviour. Image is what audiences actually perceive and remember. The relationship is directional but lossy: identity passes through touchpoints, competitors, word of mouth and cultural noise on its way to becoming image. Olins framed corporate identity work as managing the four vectors of product, environment, communication and behaviour so that image converges on identity.

Practical consequences:

- Every brand project should measure the gap between intended identity and perceived image (that gap is exactly what a [brand audit](brand-discovery.md) surfaces).
- Consistency exists to close the gap: the more coherent the touchpoints, the less noise distorts identity into image.
- Designers own identity; audiences own image. Feedback that "the audience doesn't read it the way we intended" is data about image, not a reason to redo the identity at random — but it is a reason to revisit the identity if the gap is large.

### 3.3 Brand positioning

Positioning is the deliberate choice of a place in the audience's mind relative to competitors: for whom, against what alternatives, on what difference, with what proof. It is defined precisely and operationalised in [Brand strategy](brand-strategy.md). The consequence for design is that positioning sets the *frame* the identity must perform in: an identity for the premium end of a category must look different from the category average on the axes the audience uses to judge premium-ness; an identity for a value brand must not over-design into premium cues it cannot honour.

### 3.4 Brand personality

Personality is the set of human characteristics attributed to a brand — the "if this brand were a person" construct, formalised by Jennifer Aaker into five measurable dimensions (sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness; see [Brand personality](brand-personality.md)). Its design consequence is that abstract human traits translate systematically into concrete visual parameters: colour temperature, type contrast, shape language, image style and density. Personality is the bridge document between strategy and every visual decision that follows.

### 3.5 Brand promise

The promise is the consistent expectation of value the brand commits to deliver at every encounter — the functional and emotional "what you get from us, every time". It differs from a tagline (a public expression of positioning) and from positioning (the internal strategic claim). The promise constrains design in a specific way: the identity must be sustainable across every touchpoint it is applied to, including the unglamorous ones. A brand whose identity is operatic but whose invoice template and error messages are careless has broken its own promise at the touchpoints where trust is actually tested.

### 3.6 Brand values

Values are the principles the organisation operates by — internal decision rules that also become public character. Two cautions. First, values only become brand content when they are *operational* (they change decisions); generic value lists ("integrity, quality, innovation") produce generic design because they distinguish nothing. Second, values are organisational, not design parameters — the design consequence is indirect: values inform the brief's priorities and the behaviour of the organisation (which Olins counts as an identity vector in its own right).

### 3.7 Brand purpose

Purpose is why the organisation exists beyond making money — the contribution it makes to its users or the world, articulated as a direction rather than a slogan. Purpose influences identity at the level of emphasis and subject matter: what the imagery celebrates, what the tone gravitates towards, which applications matter most. Treat claims of purpose skeptically during discovery: where purpose is asserted but not evidenced in behaviour, the design system should not amplify the claim, because audiences punish perceived inauthenticity.

### 3.8 Audience

The audience is the set of people whose perception constitutes the brand — segments with distinct needs, contexts and cultural codes. Audience definition is a research task (see [Brand discovery](brand-discovery.md)); its design consequence is *appropriateness*: visual codes are read differently by different groups (age, profession, culture, category fluency). An identity can be distinctive and well-made and still fail if it speaks the wrong visual dialect for its audience. Judging appropriateness requires evidence from the audience, not the design team's self-projection.

### 3.9 Differentiation and distinctiveness

Differentiation is the claim of being meaningfully different on attributes the audience values; distinctiveness is the ability to be noticed and recognised at all. The two are related but separable, and both matter. An identity can be differentiated on paper (a unique positioning) yet visually indistinct (looks like every competitor); or highly distinctive yet strategically generic. Evidence from marketing science (Sharp's work on distinctive brand assets, [canonical]) suggests recognition-critical assets — marks, colour, type, style — earn their keep through consistent, unique deployment over time. The design consequence: at least one element of the system should be *ownable* — something competitors cannot use without looking like a copy.

### 3.10 Brand recognition

Recognition is the audience's ability to identify the brand from partial cues: the mark without the name, the colour without the mark, the typeface, the photo style, even the layout grid. It is built by repetition of consistent cues across time and touchpoints. Practical consequences: identity systems should specify how cues combine and how they behave alone (a mark that only works accompanied by its full lockup and palette is recognition-fragile); guidelines protect recognition by restricting unauthorised variation; recognition is testable (five-second tests, degraded-display tests — see the evaluation checklist below).

### 3.11 Brand equity

Equity is the value embedded in audience perception and behaviour — awareness, associations, perceived quality, loyalty and other proprietary assets, per David Aaker's model in *Managing Brand Equity* (1991). Equity accrues from consistent, distinctive, credible brand experience and is destroyed by drift, inconsistency and broken promises. This is the economic justification for the otherwise "fussy" parts of brand work: usage rules, asset management and governance are equity-protection mechanisms, not bureaucratic decoration. For design decisions, equity has one blunt implication: rebuilding a system for aesthetic novelty destroys accumulated recognition, which is a cost that must be justified by a real strategic gain — the standard argument for evolving rather than replacing existing identities.

### 3.12 How the terms interlock

| Term | Lives where | Question it answers | Primary design consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand | Audience's mind | What do people feel and expect? | Everything in the kit shapes it; nothing in the kit *is* it |
| Logo | Owned assets | How is the source identified? | One element of the system; constrains the system when fixed |
| Identity | Organisation's output | What do we deliberately project? | The designed system itself |
| Image | Audience's perception | What do people actually perceive? | Audits measure the gap; consistency closes it |
| Positioning | Strategy | For whom, versus what, on what difference? | Sets the visual frame and category cues |
| Personality | Strategy | If the brand were a person? | Translates into colour, type, shape, imagery parameters |
| Promise | Strategy / operations | What do we deliver every time? | Identity must hold across mundane touchpoints |
| Values | Organisation | What rules do we decide by? | Informs priorities and behaviour; weak as direct design input |
| Purpose | Organisation | Why do we exist beyond profit? | Sets subject-matter emphasis and tone gravitation |
| Audience | Research | Who perceives this? | Sets appropriateness and visual dialect |
| Differentiation | Strategy | Why choose us over alternatives? | At least one ownable, meaningfully different element |
| Recognition | Perception mechanics | Can I identify it from partial cues? | Consistent, degradable cues; guidelines protect them |
| Equity | Market/finance | What is the accumulated value? | Justifies consistency rules and cautious evolution |

### 3.13 Terms commonly confused with each other

Adjacent vocabulary is used interchangeably in ordinary speech, and the confusion has real costs (teams budget for the wrong deliverable, critique judges the wrong layer). The table fixes the boundaries used throughout this repository:

| Term | What it is | What it is not | Typical artefact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand | The perception in the audience's mind | Not any single asset or document | — (measured, not drawn) |
| Branding | The activity of shaping that perception | Not the logo file; not decoration only | Strategy, identity system, guidelines |
| Brand identity | The deliberately designed expression | Not the same as image (what is actually perceived) | Visual and verbal system |
| Brand image | The perception actually held | Not controllable directly, only influenced | Audit and research findings |
| Logo | One identification asset | Not the brand, not the kit | Mark files and usage rules |
| Visual identity | The visual layer of the identity system | Not the whole identity (voice and behaviour are also identity) | Palette, type, imagery rules |
| Positioning | The strategic claim in the competitive frame | Not a tagline, not a mood | Positioning statement |
| Marketing | The activity of promoting and selling | Not the brand; marketing spends equity, brand-building earns it | Campaigns, plans |

A quick diagnostic when a meeting slides into confusion: ask which of these the current discussion is about. "We don't like the logo" may actually be "our image is weaker than competitors" (an equity problem) or "our positioning has not been decided" (a strategy problem) — each has a different remedy.

## 4. How it works

### 4.1 The perception loop

Brand-building works as a loop. Strategy defines what the organisation intends to be (positioning, personality, promise). Identity encodes that intent into designed assets. Touchpoints deliver the assets into experience. Experience becomes image in the audience's memory. Measurement (audits, research) compares image to intent and feeds corrections back into strategy and identity. Each cycle either accumulates equity (when identity, experience and image align) or erodes it (when they diverge). Understanding the loop explains the sequencing of the [brand kit procedure](../methodology/research-and-discovery.md): research precedes strategy, strategy precedes identity, identity precedes applications, and testing closes the loop before rollout.

The loop, stage by stage, with the characteristic failure at each stage:

| Stage | What happens | Characteristic failure | What detects it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy | Intent is defined | No real choice made (taste posing as strategy) | The brief's quality tests |
| Identity | Intent is encoded in assets | Encoding contradicts the intent (off-tone colour, type) | Translation-table audit |
| Touchpoints | Assets reach experience | System fails on mundane or degraded uses | Application and degradation testing |
| Image | Experience becomes perception | Perception diverges from intent | Audit, perception research |
| Measurement | Gap is fed back | No measurement at all — drift is silent | The governance loop itself |

Reading the table the other way is equally useful: any observed brand problem can be located in a stage, which usually points to the right remedy — and prevents the reflex of redrawing the logo for every ailment.

### 4.2 Why consistency does the heavy lifting

Perception is statistical, not single-shot. A single coherent encounter with a brand moves image a little; repetition of coherent encounters moves it a lot, and repetition of *incoherent* encounters actively blurs it. This is why brand guidelines specify not just the assets but their rules of combination — and why the evaluation of any applied design asks "does this look like the same organisation?" before it asks "is this application clever?". Distinctiveness plus consistency is the compound-interest mechanism behind equity; neither works without the other.

### 4.3 How concepts drive the contents of a brand kit

Concretely, the fundamental definitions determine kit contents:

1. **Brand ≠ logo** → the kit contains a system, not a mark: logo variants, clear-space and minimum-size rules, colour palette with roles, type family with hierarchy, graphic language, imagery direction, voice and tone, layout rules.
2. **Identity must be degradable** → every asset is specified in reduced conditions (single colour, small size, low-resolution).
3. **Image is measurable** → the kit includes or references measurement: audit schedule, perception baselines.
4. **Equity is protected** → the kit includes governance: who approves deviations, how assets are versioned and distributed.
5. **Personality guides application** → the kit includes examples of the system applied to typical artefacts, so tone survives contact with real use.

### 4.4 The recognition cue hierarchy

Audiences do not identify a brand from the logo alone. Each element of the system carries a share of the recognition load, and the shares differ by how unique the element is and how consistently it is deployed. A practical hierarchy, from strongest to most ambient cues:

| Cue | Recognition strength | Example of carrying the brand alone |
|---|---|---|
| Logo / mark | Strongest, but often smallest on the page | App icon at 24 px |
| Signature colour | Very strong when ownable (a colour the category does not own) | A poster in the brand colour with no mark |
| Typeface / typography style | Strong when distinctive or unusual | Body copy set in the brand face, no mark |
| Graphic language (shapes, patterns, illustration) | Strong when consistently used | Pattern-only packaging |
| Imagery style | Ambient; accumulates over exposure | Photo treatment without any mark |
| Layout and composition habits | Ambient; felt more than seen | A feed where every post has the same structure |
| Verbal signature (naming, tone, phrasing) | Strong in voice-first channels | An email that is recognisable before the sender line |

Two consequences follow. First, the kit must specify the *ambient* cues, not just the mark: colour roles, imagery treatment and layout habits are what make the brand recognisable in contexts where the logo is tiny or absent. Second, ownability matters most where the cue is strongest — a signature colour the whole category already uses carries little recognition value no matter how consistently it is applied (this is the differentiation–recognition interaction: the cue must be both unique and repeated).

### 4.5 The degradation ladder

Because identity must survive imperfect conditions, every asset is tested down a ladder of degradation until it stops working; the last working rung defines the asset's safe usage envelope:

1. Full-colour, full-size, on approved backgrounds.
2. Reduced size (typical floor: still legible at 16–24 px for digital icons; a conventional starting point — test the actual smallest real use).
3. Single colour (one ink, or dark mode).
4. Reversed (light on dark).
5. No logo at all: does the page still read as the brand through colour, type, layout and imagery alone?

Rung 5 is the honest test of a system rather than a logo. If the brand disappears when the mark does, the system is a frame around one asset, and every touchpoint where the mark is small (social avatars, favicons, sponsor lines, embroidery) is a recognition leak.

## 5. Practical application

### 5.1 Using the vocabulary in a kickoff

A practical kickoff script for a brand project, ordered by dependency: What is the purpose and the promise (organisation)? Who is the audience (research)? Who do we compete with and how are we different (positioning)? What personality must the identity express (attributes)? What already exists — logo, palette, guidelines — and what is fixed (asset audit)? What must this system do in its first year (communication objectives)? Each question has a dedicated home in [Brand discovery](brand-discovery.md) and [Brand strategy](brand-strategy.md).

### 5.2 The fixed-logo case

The signature use case for this library is extending an already-fixed event logo into a complete brand kit. The fundamentals dictate how that case differs from a full rebrand:

- The logo is treated as the primary evidence of personality: its letterforms, weights, geometry, colour and age are read as data (a rounded, friendly mark and a sharp geometric one imply different systems even for the same event). The systematic method for reading a fixed mark is covered in [Fixed logo analysis](fixed-logo-analysis.md).
- Positioning and personality work happens *after* and *with respect to* the logo analysis, not before: the strategy must not contradict the mark the audience already recognises (equity already exists in it).
- The kit's originality must come from the *system* around the fixed mark — palette roles, graphic language, imagery, layout — since the mark itself is not available for redesign.
- The end-to-end workflow for this case is specified in [Visual identity development](visual-identity.md) and the repository's brand-kit procedure.

### 5.3 Judging work with fundamentals

In critique, each fundamental becomes a testable question: Is the identity appropriate to the audience and category? Is at least one element ownable? Would a viewer recognise the brand from a partial cue? Does the application hold the promise at unglamorous touchpoints? Is anything in the work inconsistent with the strategy documents? Where evidence is missing (no audience research, no audit), flag the decision as assumption-driven rather than dressing it as strategic.

### 5.4 A minimal brand kit inventory (fixed-logo case)

The fundamentals translate into a concrete checklist of what a minimum viable kit contains when the logo is fixed. Anything on this list that is missing is a known gap to be stated, not silently skipped:

1. Logo package: the fixed mark in all existing variants, with source files, minimum sizes, clear space, and monochrome/reversed versions.
2. Colour palette: primary, secondary and accent with assigned roles and accessibility-checked combinations (including the combinations actually used by the logo).
3. Typography: text and display families (licensed and technically usable on the required platforms), with a hierarchy example.
4. Graphic language: at least one ownable device (pattern, shape, rule, texture) derived from the mark or the brand story.
5. Imagery direction: subject, treatment and avoid-list, with example images.
6. Voice and tone: voice position, tone rules for high-stakes contexts, example rewrites.
7. Layout system: a grid or set of composition rules with at least one template per priority application.
8. Application examples: the first-priority artefacts fully executed, tested per §9 below.
9. Governance: where assets live, who approves deviations, how changes are versioned.

### 5.5 Reviewing a kit someone else produced

The fundamentals also run in reverse: when you are handed an existing kit (inherited event brand, agency deliverable, previous volunteer's work), use the concepts as the review axes instead of aesthetics:

1. **Completeness against the inventory** (§5.4): which of the nine components exist, and which are gaps? Missing voice/imagery direction is the most common and most consequential gap.
2. **Degradation**: run the ladder (§4.5) on the delivered assets. Kits that were never tested at rung 3–5 usually fail there.
3. **Coherence**: reverse-engineer the personality (see [Brand personality](brand-personality.md), the reverse-engineering example) — if the parameters imply contradictory traits, the kit will feel unstable no matter how polished each part is.
4. **Constraint compliance**: does the work respect the fixed assets and the accessibility floor it claims?
5. **Evidence trail**: can any strategic claim in the kit be traced to research, or is the accompanying document preference prose? Mark untraceable claims as assumptions.

The output of this review is the same artefact as a discovery audit (see [Brand discovery](brand-discovery.md)): findings ranked by impact and effort. Treating "inherited kit review" and "discovery audit" as one procedure with two triggers keeps the method reusable across projects.

## 6. Decision framework

When making or reviewing brand decisions, run this sequence:

1. **Separate the registers.** Is the question about strategy (what to be), identity (how to look and sound), or image (how it landed)? Mixing registers — e.g. fixing a positioning problem by redrawing a logo — is the most common failure mode.
2. **Check the constraint set.** What is fixed (logo, name, legal marks, existing equity)? What is free? A decision that violates a fixed constraint is wrong regardless of its aesthetic merit.
3. **Test against the audience.** Every visual dialect choice must have an audience rationale, not only a designer rationale.
4. **Find the ownable element.** If nothing in the proposal is distinctive and ownable, the system will not accumulate recognition, however tidy it is.
5. **Stress-test the promise.** Apply the proposed identity mentally to the three most mundane artefacts in the organisation. If the system only looks right on the hero applications, it is not yet a system.
6. **Prefer evolution over replacement** when equity exists, unless the strategy has genuinely changed.

**The status of the guidance in this file.** Not everything above carries the same authority, and being able to say which is which is part of brand literacy:

- **Established concepts** (definitions in §3): the brand/identity/image distinction, positioning, personality, equity — these are the discipline's shared theoretical base, associated with named published work (Neumeier, Olins, Aaker, Wheeler).
- **Professional conventions** (the cue hierarchy, the degradation ladder, the kit inventory): widely adopted practice, sensible defaults that a specific project may justifiably depart from — the departure should then be argued, not silent.
- **Context-dependent recommendations** (the decision framework's ordering, evolution-over-replacement): correct in the typical case, inverted in real cases (the low-equity example in §7).
- **Subjective preference** (any claim of the form "this colour feels right"): not guidance at all until it is translated into a strategy or audience argument.

When writing critique or defending a decision, name the category you are invoking. "The convention is to test at 16 px, and here our smallest real use is 20 px, so we test at 20" is a design argument; "I think it looks better" is a preference that will lose to the next preference.

## 7. Examples

- **Bottled water, three positionings** (from the HBS Online teaching example): Voss targets upscale consumers with pure artesian water in an iconic glass bottle; Perrier targets middle-class buyers wanting affordable elegance, with naturally carbonated water and a design-heritage bottle story; Ethos targets socially conscious buyers by donating five cents per bottle to clean-water programmes. Same category, same product physics — three positionings produce three entirely different identity and packaging directions. This is the cleanest demonstration that positioning, not product, drives design.
- **Event brand with a fixed mark** (synthesis example, no invented facts): an annual festival has a decade-old logo the committee will not change. The fundamentals say: audit the mark (geometry, colour, recognisable features), let its character constrain personality, and build distinctiveness in the surrounding system — a palette derived from the mark, a graphic language extracted from its construction grid, imagery and layout rules that make every poster recognisable even where the logo is small. Recognition is preserved; distinctiveness is rebuilt around the mark.
- **Identity versus image drift** (common pattern): an organisation's guidelines specify a restrained, corporate visual tone, but its social media team posts loud, meme-heavy graphics. Both are "the brand's identity" in the loose sense; in the strict sense the social output has become part of the image that contradicts the intended identity. The fix is not scolding the social team but either updating the identity to absorb that register or enforcing the system at that touchpoint.
- **Weak equity, honest strategy** (pattern): a small community organisation has no meaningful recognition to preserve — the audit finds inconsistent use nobody remembers and an unremarkable mark. Here the fundamentals cut the other way: because equity is negligible, the cost of a clean rebuild is low, and the organisation should be advised that evolution arguments (§3.11) do not apply. Recognising which situation you are in — equity worth protecting versus equity not worth the constraint — is itself a fundamental judgement, made from audit evidence, not from aesthetic appetite for novelty or for preservation.
- **Strong mark, broken promise** (negative pattern): a service brand with an excellent, well-recognised mark, but where the booking flow, the complaint handling and the invoice all feel like different companies. No redesign helps, because the failure is not in the identity — it is in the promise delivery. The fundamentals give the diagnosis vocabulary: identity is coherent, image is deteriorating, the gap is produced by experience rather than by design. The remedy is operational (fix the experiences or bring them into the system's scope), which is exactly why the brand kit procedure ends with governance rather than with the logo files.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Treating the logo as the brand.** Spending the entire budget on the mark and shipping applications with no voice, imagery or layout direction. The perception is formed by the sum of touchpoints.
- **Confusing values lists with strategy.** Producing "innovation, quality, integrity" and expecting it to inform design. It cannot; nothing in it is differentiating.
- **Designing to self-image instead of audience.** The team projects its own taste onto the audience without research. Appropriateness is an empirical question.
- **Chasing novelty over recognition.** Redesigning established assets for freshness, discarding equity, then re-spending years rebuilding recognition.
- **Letting the gap between identity and image grow silently.** No audits, no perception measurement, so drift is discovered only when sales or reputation problems appear.
- **Using "branding" to mean decoration.** Applying the identity layer onto a product or event whose actual experience contradicts it — audiences resolve the conflict against the design.
- **Conflating the registers in critique.** Judging a positioning problem ("we attract the wrong audience") by redesigning the logo, or judging a logo by whether it looks like a competitor's strategy. Each register has its own evidence and its own fix.
- **Kit as logo dump.** Delivering a "brand kit" that contains logo files and nothing else. It fails the degradation ladder at rung 5 and the promise test at the first mundane artefact.
- **Treating vocabulary as pedantry.** Letting meetings proceed with "brand", "identity" and "logo" used interchangeably, so requirements, budgets and critiques each aim at different layers. The §3.13 table exists precisely so this can be corrected in one sentence.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- [ ] Can the team state what the brand promise is, and would three stakeholders state it the same way?
- [ ] Is there a defined audience beyond "everyone", with evidence behind the definition?
- [ ] Is the positioning explicit (for whom, against what, on what difference, with what proof)?
- [ ] Does the identity contain at least one distinctive, ownable element?
- [ ] Do assets survive degradation: monochrome, small size, low resolution?
- [ ] Is the gap between intended identity and perceived image measured or at least discussed?
- [ ] Does the system hold on unglamorous touchpoints (forms, footers, error states, tickets)?
- [ ] Are recognition cues consistent across the last ten published artefacts?
- [ ] Where equity exists, has the work evolved it rather than discarded it?
- [ ] Is anything being presented as "strategic" that is actually unsupported assumption?
- [ ] Do the ambient cues (colour, type, layout, imagery) carry recognition on their own, with the mark removed?
- [ ] Is there a single source of truth for assets, and does it contain what is actually in use?
- [ ] When critique happens, are claims labelled as established principle, convention, contextual recommendation or preference?
