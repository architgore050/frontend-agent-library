# Brand discovery

## 1. What is it?

Brand discovery is the structured research phase at the start of a brand project: the procedure for learning everything that the subsequent strategy and design work depends on. It covers researching the organisation, understanding its audience, analysing competitors, interviewing stakeholders, auditing the existing brand, inventorying existing assets, and defining what the brand communication must achieve. Its output is a discovery summary — evidence and constraints written down before any design begins.

Discovery is not a formality. In the brand kit procedure, it supplies the raw material for every later step: the audit defines what is fixed, the audience research defines appropriateness, competitor analysis defines where distinctiveness must come from, and stakeholder interviews define the political and operational realities the system must survive. Skipping discovery does not remove these questions — it just means they get answered by assumption, mid-project, by whoever complains loudest.

## 2. Why does it matter?

Three failure modes are prevented directly by discovery. First, **taste-driven redesign**: without an audit and audience research, a new identity is a matter of preference and inherits the designer's blind spots. Second, **broken equity**: without an inventory of existing assets, work discards accumulated recognition — most painfully in the fixed-logo case, where the whole task is to extend rather than replace. Third, **stakeholder ambush**: without interviews, the first serious objection to a design arrives after the design is finished, from someone whose requirement was never captured.

Discovery also disciplines the strategy work that follows. A positioning statement drafted without competitor analysis tends to claim a difference competitors already own; a personality defined without audience research tends to describe the founders rather than the people the brand must move. The discovery phase is where these errors are cheap to fix.

For an AI agent the value is even more concrete: discovery produces the checklists and constraint lists that an agent can execute against mechanically — the audit checklist, the interview question set, the objectives list. It converts an open-ended creative brief into a finite set of requirements.

## 3. Core principles

### 3.1 Triangulate

No single source is trusted alone. Organisation documents say what the organisation believes about itself; audiences reveal what it actually is; competitors show what the category sounds like; stakeholders reveal the politics and constraints. Findings are only treated as settled when at least two independent source types agree. Where sources conflict, the conflict is itself a finding — often the most useful one.

### 3.2 Separate facts, perceptions and wishes

Discovery artefacts are full of all three. "The logo has 14 published variants" is a fact. "Audiences under 30 find our tone dated" is a perception (possibly supported, possibly not). "We want to feel more premium" is a wish. Each is recorded with its type. Wishes are legitimate inputs to strategy, but they must not masquerade as evidence, because wishes that survive into the brief as "facts" produce designs defended against reality.

### 3.3 Interview for context, not requirements

Stakeholders are not users. Their value is context — history, constraints, success criteria, politics — not feature lists. The Nielsen Norman Group frames the stakeholder interview as "a conversation with a person who has a vested interest in a project with a goal of gathering insights to drive the project's success", best done early, one-to-one, semi-structured, with a discussion guide rather than a script. The practical consequence: good brand stakeholder questions are open-ended and diagnostic ("what does success look like?", "what has been tried before?"), not directive ("should the logo be blue?").

### 3.4 Audit the artefacts, not the intentions

A brand audit examines what actually exists — every published logo variant, every colour actually used, every piece of live copy — not what the guidelines claim exists. The gap between the two (the audit calls one version "version drift") is usually the single most informative discovery output: it shows where the system is failing to serve reality, and it defines the baseline the new system must beat.

### 3.5 Fixed assets change the questions, not the procedure

When a logo (or another element) is fixed, the discovery procedure is the same; what changes is the emphasis. The audit reads the fixed asset closely as data (construction, geometry, colour, era, recognisability); interviews focus on what audiences currently associate with it; competitor analysis focuses on how the identity must differentiate in the space *around* the fixed mark. The fixed asset is a constraint to be honoured and exploited, not a limitation to be ignored.

### 3.6 Work within consent and proportionality

Discovery involves people's time, opinions and sometimes personal data, and the procedural rules are simple enough to state here: say what the research is for; agree how notes will be attributed before recording anything; collect only what could change a decision; and treat audience data (analytics, member lists, survey responses) according to whatever data-protection obligations the organisation already has, which the organisation itself should confirm rather than the designer assume. Proportionality is the companion rule — research effort should scale to the decision at stake (§6), both to respect participants' time and to keep the project's cost honest.

## 4. How it works

The procedure below is ordered; steps 1–2 can run in parallel, and the outputs feed strategy ([Brand strategy](brand-strategy.md)) and personality work ([Brand personality](brand-personality.md)).

### Step 0 — Frame the project

Before any research, write down the frame: what is in scope and out of scope; which assets are fixed before the project starts (a fixed logo changes emphasis, not sequence — see §3.5); who the decision makers are and at which checkpoints they approve; the deadline for discovery itself (time-boxed, so the work prioritises what is load-bearing); and how findings will be recorded (the summary structure in §5.3). Framing first prevents the two classic waste patterns: researching things that cannot change any decision, and discovering constraints after the work is done. It also produces the consent ground rules for research: what will be recorded, how people will be attributed, and who sees the notes — settled in advance, not improvised in an interview.

### Step 1 — Research the organisation

Collect and read: mission, vision and values documents; business plan and recent reports; product or programme descriptions; existing guidelines; press and news coverage; organisation chart (who owns brand decisions); history and origin story. Extract: purpose claims, promise claims, values lists, tone samples, past identity decisions and why they were made. For events and institutions, add: previous editions' materials, attendance or participation figures where published, and sponsor or partner lists (they constrain visual territory — sponsors have their own identity systems that must coexist).

### Step 2 — Understand the audience

Assemble from whatever evidence exists: current audience descriptions, analytics, survey results, membership or attendee data; run new research only where the existing material is silent. Establish per segment: needs and motivations, context of encounter (where and when they meet the brand), current alternatives they consider, vocabulary they use, and any known visual-dialect facts (category conventions they expect, cultural considerations). Each evidence source answers a different question and carries a different caution:

| Source | What it tells you | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Analytics and attendance data | Who actually shows up, when, from where | Says nothing about why, or about who is *not* showing up |
| Surveys | Self-reported attitudes at scale | Stated preferences differ from observed behaviour; watch question wording |
| Interviews | Motivations, vocabulary, context in depth | Small samples; useful for hypotheses, not for prevalence claims |
| Reviews and public commentary | Unprompted language and recurring complaints | Skews towards the very satisfied and the very dissatisfied |
| Frontline staff | The questions and complaints they hear daily | Filtered through staff interpretation; verify against sources above |
| Existing personas or market reports | Prior segmentation work | Often stale or aspirational; check the date and the evidence behind them |

Where new research is needed, keep it proportionate: a handful of audience interviews plus a review of public commentary often suffices to correct internal assumptions. The general technique background lives in [Research and discovery methods](../methodology/research-and-discovery.md).

### Step 3 — Analyse competitors

Define the competitive frame from the audience's viewpoint (including non-obvious alternatives — for an event, "staying at home" competes too). Collect a visual sample of each real competitor: mark, palette, typography, imagery, voice. Record per competitor: apparent positioning, dominant visual cues, degree of consistency, and where the space is crowded versus open. Output: a competitor matrix (rows = competitors, columns = the attributes the audience uses to compare) plus a short list of "visual territory to avoid" and "visual territory that is open".

The matrix looks like this in practice (illustrative values, filled from the visual samples and audience language, not from guessing):

| Competitor | Apparent positioning | Dominant cues | Consistency | Crowded/open |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large city food fest | Premium urban lifestyle | Deep navy + gold, elegant serif, studio food photography | High | Occupied |
| County show | Tradition and agriculture | Heraldic marks, muted greens, dense schedules | Medium | Occupied |
| Street-food market series | Youthful, casual | Bright clashing colour, hand-lettering, Instagram-first | Low | Occupied but unstable |
| **Open territory** | Documentary warmth on cream/ink | — | — | **Open** |

Two disciplines keep the analysis honest: the columns mirror the attributes the audience actually uses (collected in Step 2), and every cell is evidenced with a screenshot or file link, so the matrix can be re-checked rather than remembered.

### Step 4 — Interview stakeholders

Run semi-structured one-to-one interviews (NN/g's recommended format) with the brand owner, senior leadership, the people who will operate the system daily, and representative audience-facing staff. Number is governed by the goal: five to eight stakeholders typically saturate the useful themes for a brand project; NN/g notes that insights from five or more interviewees can be analysed thematically, coding observations to find recurring themes. The full question set is the template in §5.1.

### Step 5 — Audit the brand

Follow the audit checklist in §5.2. Score findings by impact and effort (the Splash Creative audit's practical scoring method: rate each finding 1–3 on each dimension, then prioritise high-impact/low-effort items first). The audit covers visual identity consistency, verbal identity, digital presence, perception versus intention, and competitor benchmarking — the same six areas Splash Creative's checklist organises, adapted here for brand-kit work.

### Step 6 — Inventory existing brand assets

Physically gather: every logo file and variant in circulation (source files where they exist); font licenses and files; colour specifications (and the colours actually in use); photography and illustration libraries; templates; domain and social handles; merchandise, signage and any legacy artwork. Record per asset: version, format, owner, license status, and whether it is fixed, deprecated or open for redesign. This inventory is what makes the "fixed logo" workflow possible — before designing anything, the agent knows exactly what exists, in what quality, with what permissions.

### Step 7 — Define communication objectives

Convert everything above into explicit objectives: what the brand communication must achieve, for which audience, by which measure. Good objectives are behavioural and checkable ("be identifiable from a distance of five metres on social banners", "be distinguishable from the two nearest competitor events at a glance on a feed", "carry the kit to merchandise and wayfinding without a logo present"), not aspirations ("look more professional"). These objectives become the success criteria section of the design brief and the evaluation checklist used at the end of the project.

## 5. Practical application — the three templates

### 5.1 Stakeholder interview question set

Use semi-structured; adapt wording; ask "why" and "can you give me an example" probes. Structure follows NN/g's question categories (role, success, priorities, history, process) extended with brand-specific sets.

**Role and context (opening)**

1. Can you tell me a little about your role and how you relate to the brand?
2. In your own words, what is this organisation, and who is it for?
3. What is your ideal level of involvement in this project, and how do you prefer to communicate?

**Success and objectives**

4. What does success look like for you and your team — this year, and after this project?
5. What is the one thing we must get right for this project to be worth undertaking?
6. How will you personally judge whether the new brand identity has worked?
7. What challenges or issues are currently top priorities for you?

**History and politics**

8. Can you tell me about any previous attempts to change or improve the brand — what happened, and why did it succeed or miss the mark?
9. Are there assets or elements people are attached to (the logo, a colour, an image style)? Why do they matter to people?
10. Are there decisions about the brand that are already fixed before we start?
11. Who else's opinion will matter when this is approved, and what will they look for?

**Audience and perception**

12. Who do you believe the brand speaks to, and who would you like it to speak to that it currently does not?
13. What do people currently say about us — the words you hear them use?
14. Where does the brand encounter its audience, and which of those encounters matter most?

**Competitors and difference**

15. Who do audiences actually compare us with?
16. Why does someone choose us instead of them — and why do some choose them instead of us?
17. What do you admire in other brands' identities, inside or outside our category — and what would you never copy?

**Operations and close**

18. What artefacts does the brand need to survive on day one? (ticket, website, poster, app icon, signage, merchandise…)
19. What has made past guidelines or asset libraries hard to use?
20. Who else should I speak to?

A small bank of probes for the moments when an answer stays at the surface; each probe is attached to a question above:

- "Can you walk me through the last time that happened?" (converts opinion into a specific episode)
- "When you say 'modern', what would you point at?" (decodes visual adjectives into comparable references)
- "Who does that well, in your view?" (surfaces the comparison set people actually hold)
- "What would have to be true for you to change your mind?" (exposes the real constraint behind a stance)
- "If we did nothing at all, what happens?" (tests whether the project has a real driver)

Interview logistics (from NN/g's guidance): one-to-one, thirty to sixty minutes, with the purpose stated in the invitation; a discussion guide rather than a script; open probes throughout ("can you give me an example?", "why is that important to you?"); close by asking who else to speak to; and send a short thank-you with an offer to correct any mis-recorded statement. Take notes with attribution by role, and distinguish verbatim quotes from paraphrase — verbatim audience and stakeholder language is later raw material for personality attributes.

### 5.2 Brand audit checklist

Work through every area; record evidence (screenshots, files, links) for each finding; score each finding impact 1–3 and effort 1–3; prioritise impact ÷ effort.

| Area | Check items |
|---|---|
| Goals and scope | What decision will this audit inform? Time-box it; an unfocused audit drags on |
| Visual identity | Logo variants in circulation (count them; flag stretched, recoloured, outdated versions); palette adherence (are official colours actually used?); typography consistency; clear-space and size violations; single-colour and small-size survivability |
| Verbal identity | Voice consistency across homepage, social bios, email footers, sales decks; tagline in use — current or abandoned?; value proposition stated consistently? |
| Digital presence | Site performance; mobile behaviour; content gaps; search visibility; social profile completeness and consistency; AI-assistant descriptions of the brand (what do assistants currently say?) |
| Perception | How audiences describe the brand (surveys, reviews, social listening) versus intended positioning; the gap is where the brief usually lives |
| Competitive benchmark | 3–5 competitors scored on the same criteria: visual clarity, messaging consistency, digital performance, positioning |
| Governance | Do usable guidelines exist? Are assets distributed from a single source? Who approves deviations? |
| Environment and physical | Signage, merchandise, uniforms, vehicle livery, event stands: do physical applications follow the system, and can the system survive production constraints (spot colour, embroidery, engraving)? |
| Degradation survival | Sample assets tested monochrome, reversed, small-size; where does recognition fail first? |
| Templates in use | Documents, presentations, email footers, tickets: are staff working from templates or reinventing per artefact? |

### 5.3 Discovery summary structure

A discovery summary is a short document (or markdown file) that makes the research usable. Recommended structure:

1. **Project frame** — scope, what is fixed, timeline, decision makers.
2. **Organisation picture** — purpose, promise, values (with evidence), history, operational realities.
3. **Audience picture** — segments with needs and contexts; evidence and confidence level per claim; visual-dialect notes.
4. **Competitor picture** — matrix summary; crowded visual territory; open territory.
5. **Stakeholder findings** — themes with supporting quotes (attributed by role, not name, unless agreed otherwise); constraints and mandatories; known risks.
6. **Audit findings** — top findings ranked by impact ÷ effort, with evidence links; asset inventory table (fixed / deprecated / open).
7. **Communication objectives** — the checkable list that becomes the brief's success criteria.
8. **Implications for strategy and design** — the 5–10 consequences that must shape the work; open questions and assumptions flagged as assumptions.

Every claim in the summary carries its source type (fact / perception / wish) and confidence; the summary feeds directly into positioning work ([Brand strategy](brand-strategy.md)) and the moodboard brief ([Moodboards](../methodology/moodboards.md)).

As a skeleton, the summary reads like this (one line per finding in the real document; each finding carries a source and a confidence note):

```markdown
# Discovery summary — <organisation / event>
## 1. Project frame
Scope: full kit extension, logo fixed. Deadline: discovery 2 weeks, kit 6 weeks.
Decision makers: <roles>. Checkpoints: moodboard, applications, final.
## 2. Organisation
Purpose/promise (evidence: <doc>), values in use vs printed, history notes,
operational realities (who will use the kit daily, with what skills).
## 3. Audience
Segment A: needs, contexts, alternatives, vocabulary (evidence: <source>,
confidence: high/medium). Segment B: ... Visual-dialect notes: ...
## 4. Competitors
Matrix summary (attributes × competitors). Crowded territory: ...
Open territory: ...
## 5. Stakeholder findings
Theme 1 — <theme> (roles citing it: ...). Theme 2 — ...
Constraints/mandatories: ... Risks: ...
## 6. Audit findings
1. <finding> — impact 3 / effort 1 → first. 2. ... (evidence: <links>)
Asset inventory: <table — asset, version, owner, license, fixed/open>
## 7. Communication objectives
O1: <behavioural, checkable>. O2: ...
## 8. Implications for strategy and design
I1: ... I2: ... Open questions: ... Assumptions flagged: ...
```

## 6. Decision framework

**How much discovery is enough?**

1. Scale to the decision: a full kit for a multi-year brand justifies the whole procedure; a colour extension of an existing system justifies the audit and a short interview round.
2. Stop at saturation: when the last three interviews produce no new themes, interviewing more stakeholders is avoidance, not research.
3. Two-source rule: any claim that reaches the brief uncontradicted by a second source is labelled an assumption in the summary.
4. Fixed assets shift effort, not sequence: with a fixed logo, spend the saved logo-design debate on deeper audit and audience-association research.
5. Time-box explicitly: decide the deadline for discovery before starting, and let it force prioritisation of what is actually load-bearing.

**A proportionate timeline** (for a full-kit project with a fixed logo; compress or stretch to the project, never skip a step silently):

| Phase | Work | Typical effort |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1, first half | Framing (Step 0); document research (Step 1); asset inventory begins (Step 6) | concentrated desk work |
| Week 1, second half | Stakeholder interviews (Step 4); audience evidence assembly (Step 2) | 5–8 interviews scheduled in parallel |
| Week 2, first half | Competitor analysis (Step 3); audit execution and scoring (Step 5) | the audit alone fits in 3–5 business days |
| Week 2, second half | Synthesis, objectives (Step 7), summary writing, internal review | ends with the brief handover |

**Choosing the competitor set:** include the brands the audience actually names (ask them), the brands internal stakeholders fear, and one aspirational outside-category brand whose standards the work will be judged against.

## 7. Examples

- **Event with a decade-old logo** (synthesis example): audit finds the mark exists in fourteen unofficial variants (squashed, recoloured, plus a "2019 special edition" version), official colours have drifted to two different blues, and typography is whatever each year's volunteer chose. Interviews reveal the committee is attached to the mark itself but not to anything else. The discovery summary therefore fixes only the mark, lists the palette as "open but must derive from the mark's own blue", sets an objective of one-glance recognition in a crowded events feed, and ranks "single source of truth for assets" as the highest impact ÷ effort item. Every later design decision now has a documented reason.
- **Version drift as a finding** (pattern from audit practice): a brand's email templates use an off-spec colour and an outdated logo, while the website is current. The perception audit then shows audiences describe the brand with the *email* impression. The gap between guideline and reality — not aesthetic opinion — decides where the redesign effort goes first.
- **Stakeholder history preventing rework** (pattern): interviews reveal a previous rebrand was reversed within months because a senior figure was never consulted. The new project adds an early approval checkpoint with that stakeholder (captured in the discovery summary's "decision makers" section) before any design is produced — a political constraint discovered for the cost of one conversation.
- **The volunteer-run organisation** (pattern): a charity or community group where every asset was made by a different volunteer over ten years. The audit finds no guidelines, no source files, fonts from defunct websites, and photography of varying license status. The discovery summary here does something specific: it separates what must be recreated (assets without license or source), what should be preserved (anything audiences genuinely recognise), and what must be built new (the governance the organisation never had). The recommendations are then sized to the organisation's reality — a volunteer-run group cannot maintain a 90-page guideline document, so the summary recommends a short kit and a single asset folder with naming rules instead.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Auditing intentions instead of artefacts.** Reading the guidelines and believing them; the truth is in the published files.
- **Interviewing to validate.** Asking stakeholders to confirm the direction already chosen. Semi-structured, open questions exist precisely to surface what was not predicted.
- **One source, one truth.** Building the brief on a single stakeholder's opinion; the two-source rule exists to catch this.
- **Discovery theatre.** Producing 40 pages of context with no implications section; the summary must end in consequences for design.
- **Unbounded competitor analysis.** Analysing thirty competitors superficially instead of five deeply; the frame is defined by what audiences compare, not by what is easy to find.
- **Skipping the asset inventory.** Starting design before knowing which files exist, which are licensed and which are fixed — the fixed-logo case becomes impossible to manage.
- **Aspirational objectives.** Writing "look more modern" as an objective; nothing checkable follows from it, and evaluation at the end of the project has nothing to test.
- **Recording people carelessly.** Interviewing without agreed attribution rules, or circulating notes that name individuals; stakeholders stop being candid, and the project loses its best source. Agree the recording and attribution rules in the framing step and honour them.
- **Researching what cannot change a decision.** Collecting material because it is easy, not because it is load-bearing; the time-box and the framing step exist to prevent exactly this.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- [ ] Organisation research covers documents, history and decision structure, with purpose/promise claims evidenced.
- [ ] Audience description per segment includes needs, context, alternatives and vocabulary, with source and confidence per claim.
- [ ] Competitor set reflects the audience's real comparison set and includes a visual sample per competitor.
- [ ] Stakeholder interviews ran one-to-one, semi-structured, covering success, history, audience, competitors and operations.
- [ ] Interview findings are thematically coded (five or more interviewees) or otherwise systematically summarised.
- [ ] The audit covered visual identity, verbal identity, digital presence, perception, competitors and governance, with findings scored by impact and effort.
- [ ] An asset inventory exists with version, format, owner, license and fixed/open status for every asset.
- [ ] Communication objectives are behavioural and checkable.
- [ ] The discovery summary ends with implications, open questions and assumptions explicitly labelled.
- [ ] No claim in the brief rests on a single uncorroborated source without being flagged.
- [ ] Aspirations in the material are recorded as wishes, not evidence, and none reach the brief disguised as findings.
- [ ] Research recordings, notes and audience data follow the consent and attribution rules agreed in the framing step.
