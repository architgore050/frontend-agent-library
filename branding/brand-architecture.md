# Brand architecture

## 1. What is it?

Brand architecture is the organising structure that defines how a set of brands relate to one another: which brand leads in front of the audience, which appear as endorsers, which stay hidden, and how names and marks cascade from a parent organisation to its sub-brands, products, events and campaigns. Where [Visual identity](visual-identity.md) asks "what does the brand look like?", brand architecture asks "which brand is this piece of communication actually for?"

The discipline is usually described as a spectrum between two poles. A **branded house** applies one master brand across everything (services and sub-offerings share the master's name and identity). A **house of brands** keeps the corporate brand in the background while each offering carries its own independent identity. Between them lie hybrids — sub-brands, endorsed brands and co-branded arrangements — where some equity is shared and some is not. This spectrum is standard in the professional literature: David Aaker and Erich Joachimsthaler's brand relationship spectrum and Alina Wheeler's treatment of architecture both organise the field this way [canonical]; recent practitioner articles describe the same two poles in the same terms [search-verified].

## 2. Why does it matter?

Architecture determines who accumulates recognition. Every communication strengthens some brand; the question is which. If a department builds its own distinct identity, its success does not reinforce the parent; if it lives under the master brand, its failures can also damage the parent. Getting this wrong is expensive in both directions: a house-of-brands strategy multiplies design, legal and marketing costs because each brand must be built from zero, while a pure branded house can smother offerings whose audiences or personalities genuinely differ (practitioner sources note the branded house's monotone risk and the house of brands' chaos risk [search-verified]).

For events and multi-part programmes — the common case in this repository's use domain — architecture answers the everyday questions that otherwise get decided ad hoc and inconsistently: Does the 2027 edition of the festival get its own logo or a date variant of the master mark? Does the workshop series carry the parent's mark as an endorser? Where does the sponsor's logo sit, at what size, and what does that imply? Each answer is an architecture decision, and [Brand governance](brand-governance.md) enforces the ones already made.

Architecture also disciplines collaboration. Partnerships, sponsorships and endorsements all produce the same question — "whose logo goes where, and how big?" — and without a documented structure the answer is negotiated per artefact, which is slow, uneven and a recurring source of friction between organisations. A structure with tiers and lockup recipes turns that negotiation into a lookup.

## 3. Core principles

**Equity flows along the visible hierarchy.** Audience trust and recognition transfer through what they can see: a master brand transfers equity down to sub-brands; an endorsement mark transfers trust upward to an unknown name; an independent brand builds (or squanders) its own equity alone. Choose the structure by deciding deliberately where equity should flow.

**One lead brand per communication.** Every surface should have a single brand that dominates, with endorsers and partners in supporting roles. Multiple competing marks of equal size on one artefact confuse attribution — audiences cannot tell whose communication it is.

**Structure follows strategy, not ambition.** Architecture choices should come from the organisation's positioning, portfolio and resources (see [Brand strategy](brand-strategy.md)), not from each unit's desire for its own logo. Autonomy has a price; the parent's strength is the default asset.

**Temporary identities live inside permanent ones.** Campaigns, editions and one-off programmes should ride on the permanent brand's system — palette, type, graphic language — and earn distinctiveness through content and naming rather than through a competing visual identity. Permanent structure, temporary expression.

**Architecture is visible and revisable.** The structure is written down (the summary table in 5), and it has review triggers: a merger or partnership, a new audience, a major event, or a pattern of exceptions against the same slot ([Brand governance](brand-governance.md)) are all signals that the structure — not just the outputs — needs revisiting. An architecture that cannot be questioned calcifies; one that changes without process is not an architecture but a running argument.

## 4. How it works

### 4.1 The architecture spectrum

| Model | Structure | Equity effect | Cost profile | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Branded house | One master brand on everything | Full transfer both ways | Lowest — one identity | Organisations with a strong single reputation |
| Sub-brands | Master + modified child marks (colour, descriptor, lockup) | Strong transfer, some differentiation | Low–medium | Families of offerings sharing an audience |
| Endorsed brands | Independent child marks + parent endorsement ("by Parent") | Trust flows up to the child; child keeps its own personality | Medium | Units whose audience differs from the parent's |
| House of brands | Fully independent brands; parent hidden or legal-only | No transfer; insulation both ways | Highest — N full identities | Conglomerates, risk isolation, multi-audience portfolios |
| Hybrid | Different models per tier | Mixed | Mixed | Most real organisations |

Most organisations occupy a hybrid position. The design task is not to pick a slogan but to assign **every existing and future offering** a slot in the structure, and to write the assignment down. MIT provides a live example of boundary-drawing: sub-branding is permitted only for departments, labs, centres, institutes, initiatives and programmes — explicitly not for publications, events, resources or student groups [accessed]. That is architecture as explicit policy, not taste.

Choosing a slot for any given offering turns on audience and personality fit rather than org-chart position. Where the audience is the same and the personality compatible, the master brand should lead — it is the cheapest option and compounds recognition. Where the audience differs but trust must carry across, endorsement preserves the connection without forcing one personality onto both. Where the audiences conflict or the risk is isolating an association (a commercial venture, a politically sensitive programme), independence is the safe slot despite its cost. Heritage and legacy entities deserve a deliberate decision rather than a default: a long-running programme with its own loyal audience may be best endorsed rather than absorbed, precisely because its equity is older than the current master identity.

Each slot on the spectrum implies a different visual treatment, which is how the architecture becomes executable design work:

| Model | Visual expression | Logo usage |
|---|---|---|
| Branded house | One identity everywhere; offerings are named, not re-designed | Master mark on everything; descriptors in brand type |
| Sub-brands | Shared system (palette, type, grid) + differentiated descriptor or colour | Parent mark in a defined lockup recipe |
| Endorsed | Child identity leads; parent appears as a small endorser | Child mark primary; "by Parent" endorsement lockup |
| House of brands | Fully separate identities | No visible parent mark |
| Campaign layer | Master system + temporary message layer | Master mark unchanged; everything else may vary |

### 4.2 Parent and sub-brand relationships

A sub-brand relationship is expressed visually through the lockup and naming logic: the parent mark plus a differentiated descriptor ("Master Name — Unit Name"), usually with a shared element (colour, typography or a symbol fragment) that signals kinship. The system must define: which parent mark form the sub-brand uses; how the descriptor is set (typeface, case, weight); whether the sub-brand gets a symbol of its own (MIT's guidance allows a distinct symbol for custom sub-brand logos but forbids repurposing master logo components into new marks [accessed]); and what the sub-brand may never do (alter the parent mark, exceed the parent's visual weight, introduce off-palette colours — MIT restricts sub-brand logos to core colours [accessed]).

The shared element deserves one deliberate decision rather than an accident of styling. Kinship signals that survive production are the robust ones: a shared typeface, a shared palette role, a shared geometry (radius, angle) — not effects that printers, platforms and volunteers will lose first. A useful rule of thumb: if the kinship signal disappears in a single-colour photocopy of the lockup, it is too fragile to carry the relationship.

### 4.3 Event identities

Events are time-boxed brands inside a permanent organisation. The recurring architecture question is the **edition rule**: what persists across editions and what changes. The robust pattern is a persistent master identity (name, symbol, palette, type) with a per-edition variation layer (year, theme colour, campaign artwork) — the master stays recognisable while each edition feels current. An edition that rebuilds its whole identity every year never accumulates recognition; one that never varies feels stale. Decisions to fix in advance: whether the date is inside the mark or beside it; how a theme colour interacts with the master palette without becoming a rogue palette; and what happens to the event's identity when it moves venue, changes scale or gets a spin-off (spin-offs are sub-brand decisions, see 4.2).

The edition layer has a small lifecycle worth designing deliberately, because it repeats every cycle:

1. **Announcement** — master identity plus the new edition's year/date element; the variation layer is minimal, and the master does most of the work.
2. **Build-up** — campaign artwork, theme colour and motifs come online; the master mark and its rules stay fixed.
3. **Live** — full edition expression across venues, merchandise and media; this is where the temptation to alter the mark peaks, and where the architecture rule (campaigns never alter the mark) earns its keep.
4. **Wrap and archive** — edition assets are archived with their dates, templates revert to the master baseline, and the edition's lessons feed the next cycle. Archiving matters: next year's designer should find the edition's assets dated and closed, not mixed into the master library.

A date inside the mark (the year drawn into the badge) is a strong choice that maximises edition feel but forces either a redesign each cycle or an increasingly stale year; a date beside the mark is safer and keeps the mark permanent. Both are legitimate — the failure is deciding implicitly, per edition, by whoever is designing that year.

### 4.4 Product identities

Products sit below the organisational brand and inherit or decline equity deliberately. In a branded house the product is named under the master ("Master ProductLine"); in an endorsed structure it carries its own name with the parent as endorser. The visual system implication: product identities need *less* distinctiveness than they think — palette and type usually stay in the master system, and differentiation comes from naming, imagery and product-specific graphic elements. This keeps the portfolio coherent and the design cost down.

Three levels of product differentiation are worth distinguishing, because teams routinely overshoot:

1. **Descriptor level** — the product is a named offering of the parent, set entirely in the master system. Appropriate for most services and programme lines.
2. **Sub-identity level** — the product earns its own colour, icon or motif within the master system (a product colour, a product glyph in app contexts). Appropriate when the product has a distinct interface or audience.
3. **Independent level** — a full identity with its own mark. Justified only when the audience, business model or risk profile genuinely separates it from the parent — the house-of-brands logic applied to a single product.

Escalating from level 1 to level 2 is cheap and reversible; jumping to level 3 is an architecture decision with legal, design and maintenance costs that should go through the owner ([Brand governance](brand-governance.md)). A practical signal that a product is over-differentiated: users cannot tell it belongs to the organisation — the equity flow has been cut without a strategy reason.

### 4.5 Campaign identities

Campaigns are the most temporary layer: seasonal pushes, anniversaries, calls to action. The professional convention is a **campaign look inside the system**: a campaign message set in the brand's type, a campaign colour drawn from or approved against the master palette, campaign-specific imagery and motifs — but the master logo placement, clear space and layout rules unchanged. Campaigns must never alter the master mark (a temporary logo variant is an architecture decision requiring governance sign-off, not a campaign designer's liberty). Lifetime rules matter: define when the campaign ends and its assets are archived, so stale campaign visuals do not linger in templates — a drift source handled in [Brand governance](brand-governance.md).

### 4.6 Co-branding

Co-branding places two independent brands into one communication with negotiated prominence. The visual grammar is the lockup rules of [Logo systems](logo-systems.md) applied with a business overlay: who leads (Uber Eats' published co-marketing rule that the partner logo appears first and takes the visual lead is a business decision expressed visually [accessed]), the divider convention, equal or unequal sizing, and in which contexts the lockup may appear. Agree prominence in the partnership terms, not in the layout tool: the design execution must simply record the agreement.

Prominence itself is a small ladder, and the phrase attached to each rung should match the visual rank:

| Rung | Visual rank | Typical phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Title / presenting partner | Equal or near-equal, beside the host mark | "Presented by", "in partnership with" |
| Headline sponsor | 60–100% of host mark (see 4.7) | "Headline sponsor: X" |
| Supporting partner | Smaller, grouped in a strip or wall | "Supported by X" |
| Supplier / in-kind | Listing only, collective wall | "X provides…" |

The ladder prevents the most common co-branding argument — "our logo is too small" — by making size a function of the agreed rung, not of the artwork's free space. When a partner's agreement upgrades their rung, the lockup template changes with it; when it lapses, it reverts. Uber Eats' phrase policy (specific phrases reserved for exclusive opportunities, standard phrases for ordinary co-marketing [accessed]) is exactly this principle implemented in language.

### 4.7 Sponsor placement

Sponsorship is structured prominence: sponsors are tiered (headline, main, supporting, in-kind), and each tier has defined placement, size and exclusivity rules. Good practice treats sponsor placement as a documented system, not a per-poster negotiation: a tier table mapping tier → size relative to the event mark → permitted positions (title bar, sponsor strip, back cover, website footer) → contexts where the sponsor may not appear (for example inside editorial imagery or on the master mark itself). Two boundary rules prevent the common failures: the event's own mark keeps visual leadership on all owned surfaces, and sponsor logos never enter the event mark's clear space or its lockups.

A worked tier table for a mid-scale event (values are conventional starting points — adapt to the partnership agreement):

| Tier | Size relative to event mark | Positions permitted | Exclusivity typically granted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline ("Presented by") | 60–100% of event mark height | Title bar beside the event mark; all key visuals | Category exclusivity; phrase-level prominence |
| Main sponsor | 30–50% | Sponsor strip on posters, web footer, stage backdrops | Shared category exclusivity |
| Supporting sponsor | 15–25% | Collective sponsor wall, web partner page | Listing rights |
| In-kind supporter | 10–15% | Collective wall only, partner page | Listing rights |

The percentages are conventions to be fixed once, in writing, per event — the governance value is that a sponsor's designers can self-serve the rules instead of negotiating size per artwork ([Brand governance](brand-governance.md)). What the table also encodes, deliberately, is de-escalation: a lower tier never outsizes a higher one, and no tier outsizes the host.

### 4.8 Partner logos and endorsement lockups

Partner and endorsement lockups are the small mechanics of the structure: an endorser mark set above or beside a unit name at fixed relative size; a partner strip on the layout grid; "presented by" versus "in partnership with" phrasing attached to different prominence levels (Uber Eats reserves specific phrases — "Presented by" for exclusive media spotlight opportunities, standard phrases like "Order with"/"Available on" otherwise [accessed]). Language and visual prominence should match: the stronger the phrase, the stronger the visual rank. Endorsement lockups must preserve both parties' clear space, use each mark's supplied files, and align on a common axis per the co-branding rules in [Logo systems](logo-systems.md) section 7.

### 4.9 Naming hierarchy basics

Naming is architecture's verbal layer. A workable naming hierarchy keeps levels consistent: organisation → offering → edition/campaign, each level named with a consistent pattern (for example "Parent — Unit" at level two, "Unit — Edition Year" at level three). Conventions worth fixing early: descriptive versus coined names per level; whether acronyms are official; how sub-names are capitalised; and the rule that naming changes are architecture changes (they need governance approval and guideline updates — see [Brand guidelines](brand-guidelines.md)), not renaming by one team. Deep, inconsistent naming trees are the most common invisible architecture failure: the visual system can be impeccable while five different naming patterns quietly imply five different organisations.

A documented naming tree makes the structure auditable:

```text
Northfield Arts Trust            (organisation — coined + descriptive)
├── Northfield Arts Festival     (flagship event — descriptive)
│   ├── Festival 2027            (edition — year suffix)
│   └── Festival Fringe          (spin-off — sub-brand slot)
├── Northfield Studios           (venue — descriptive)
└── Trust Workshops              (programme — branded-house slot)
```

Conventions the tree fixes at a glance: level-two entities carry the parent name as a prefix (branded house); the edition is a suffix, never drawn into the mark; the spin-off ("Fringe") is a sub-brand slot with its own descriptor rather than a new organisation. When a proposed name does not fit the tree, that is an architecture conversation, not a copywriting one.

## 5. Practical application

1. **Inventory the portfolio.** List every brand-bearing entity: organisation, units, products, events, programmes, recurring campaigns, partnerships. Nothing gets a slot until it is on the list.
2. **Assign each entity a model slot** from the spectrum table (4.1), driven by audience overlap and equity strategy from [Brand strategy](brand-strategy.md).
3. **Define the visual expression per slot**: parent usage rules, sub-brand lockup recipes, endorsement lockups, event edition rules, campaign layer rules.
4. **Write the sponsor and partner tier table** if external marks are involved.
5. **Publish the architecture** as a section of the guidelines with a one-page decision tree ("You are launching X — which structure applies?"), and route new-brand requests through [Brand governance](brand-governance.md).

The published architecture summary can be a single table that future contributors can read in a minute:

```markdown
## Architecture summary

| Entity | Slot | Leads with | Inherits | Never |
|--------|------|-----------|----------|-------|
| [Parent] | Master brand | its own mark | — | — |
| [Unit] | Sub-brand | parent lockup | palette, type | own symbol without approval |
| [Event] | Event identity | event mark | master palette core | altering the master mark |
| [Campaign] | Campaign layer | master mark | full system | new permanent colours |
| [Partner] | Co-branding | per agreement | — | entering clear space |
```

One row per entity, four columns, and most architecture questions become lookups. When a new entity is proposed, it is added to this table as part of its approval — the table and the guidelines move together, and the changelog records who approved the addition.

## 6. Decision framework

When a new entity, event, product or partnership appears, decide with these questions in order:

| Question | If yes → | If no → |
|---|---|---|
| Does the entity serve the same audience and personality as the parent? | Sub-brand or branded house slot | Endorsed or independent slot |
| Must the parent's reputation carry it (or insulate the parent from it)? | Branded house / endorsement | Independent brand |
| Is the entity temporary (campaign, edition)? | Stay inside the master system; variation layer only | Permanent slot in the structure |
| Are two organisations sharing the communication? | Co-branding rules; agree prominence in writing | Single-brand layout |
| Is an external party paying for prominence? | Sponsor tier table governs size/position | Standard layout rules |

The default when genuinely uncertain is the most conservative option: keep the entity inside the master system with a descriptor, and revisit if its audience proves distinct. Reversing a branded-house decision into independence is easier than merging N independent brands back into a system.

Two worked applications of the framework. First, a community organisation adds a paid training course aimed at corporate clients: same parent, different audience — the framework routes it to an endorsed slot ("Course name, by Parent") rather than a full sub-brand, because the audience differs but the parent's credibility is the selling point. Second, a festival adds a one-off midnight cinema programme: temporary, same audience — it stays inside the master system as a named programme with campaign-layer treatment, no new mark. In both cases the framework produced a defensible answer in minutes, and the answer is written down and owned by governance rather than renegotiated per project.

## 7. Examples

**MIT** ([accessed](https://brand.mit.edu/applying-brand/do-dont)). A strongly governed hybrid: the Institute master brand with explicitly permitted sub-branding for departments, labs, centres, institutes, initiatives and programmes; an endorsed-branding pattern for units that keep their own identity ("MIT logo at top of all web pages... set 'MIT' in plain text or a complementary custom font rather than creating a new combined identity"); and hard boundary rules — no sub-brands for publications, events or student groups; no repurposing of logo components into new marks. Illustrates architecture written as enforceable policy.

**Uber Eats** ([accessed](https://merchants.ubereats.com/us/en/resources/learning-center/co-marketing-tools)). Co-branding mechanics as published policy: side-by-side partner lockups with a divider, spacing in derived units, partner-led prominence, a Brand Desk approval flow with turnaround, and message phrases graded by partnership level ("Presented by" reserved for exclusive media spotlight opportunities). Illustrates that co-branding is negotiated business structure executed through fixed visual grammar.

**University at Buffalo** ([accessed](https://www.buffalo.edu/brand/identity/usage/Clear-space.html)). A mark hierarchy — primary academic mark, master brand lockup, unit lockups, spirit mark, crest — with per-mark rules and the explicit prohibition on combining the primary mark with other words or graphics to form new marks. Illustrates one organisation managing several mark classes under one architecture.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Every unit wants its own logo.** Uncontrolled independence fragments the portfolio and multiplies cost. Fix: architecture policy defining who may have what, approved by the parent.
- **Campaigns that alter the master mark.** Temporary logo recolours or mash-ups break the identity's strongest asset. Fix: campaigns vary everything except the mark.
- **Event editions rebuilt from zero each year.** Recognition never accumulates. Fix: persistent master identity plus a defined edition layer.
- **Sponsor creep.** Sponsor logos grow until they rival the host mark. Fix: tier table with size ratios; enforce via review.
- **Endorsement phrasing stronger than the visual rank** (or vice versa): "presented by" with a tiny footnote mark, or a headline sponsor treated as a footer. Fix: phrase and visual rank must match.
- **Naming-tree drift.** Five patterns for five units' names imply different organisations. Fix: naming hierarchy conventions in the guidelines.
- **Architecture undocumented.** Decisions live in one designer's head; the next project restarts the debate. Fix: publish the architecture section and route exceptions through governance.
- **Endorsement without permission.** A unit adds "by [Parent]" to its materials, or a partner adds the organisation's mark to theirs. Endorsement implies responsibility, so it is granted, not taken. Fix: endorsement lockups exist only as approved recipes, and misuse is handled like any other governance breach.
- **Architecture amnesia after staff change.** A new coordinator "tidies up" the sponsor wall into equal-size logos, or rebrands a sub-unit. Fix: the summary table, decision log and changelog make the structure survive the people who set it up.

## 9. Evaluation checklist

- [ ] Every brand-bearing entity is assigned to a slot in the architecture (branded house / sub-brand / endorsed / independent / campaign layer).
- [ ] The assignment is written down and published, with the rationale for each major choice.
- [ ] Sub-brand lockup recipes exist: parent mark form, descriptor styling, shared elements, prohibitions.
- [ ] Event edition rules are defined before launch: what persists, what varies, where the date lives.
- [ ] Campaign rules allow variation in message, colour-within-palette and imagery — never in the mark.
- [ ] A sponsor tier table exists (tier → size ratio → positions → exclusivities) and the host mark keeps visual leadership.
- [ ] Co-branding and endorsement lockups follow [Logo systems](logo-systems.md) section 7, with prominence agreed in writing.
- [ ] Naming hierarchy conventions are defined per level and naming changes are routed through governance.
- [ ] New-brand requests have a defined decision path and approver.
- [ ] Architecture documentation is current: recent changes appear in the guideline changelog.
- [ ] The architecture summary table is the single place where every entity's slot is recorded.
- [ ] Endorsement phrases ("presented by", "supported by") are enumerated and matched to visual rank.
- [ ] Every entity that bears a mark can be traced to a slot, an owner and an approval record.
- [ ] Review triggers for revisiting the architecture are written down (partnerships, new audiences, exception patterns).
